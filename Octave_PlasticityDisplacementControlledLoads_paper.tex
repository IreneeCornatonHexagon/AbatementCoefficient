\begin{abstract}
Piping is commonly assessed using linear-elastic calculations. Because these calculations do not directly describe the local redistribution of stress and strain after yielding, their results require a posteriori interpretation, including stress classification and consideration of elastic follow-up. This effect can be important at elevated temperature: when inelastic deformation is concentrated in a limited region while most of the structure remains elastic, the surrounding structure acts as a spring and can drive substantial local deformation, even when the applied loading would otherwise be considered self-limiting. This paper examines the physical meaning of elastic follow-up and its treatment in the RCC-MRx rules, focusing on the monotonic elastic follow-up factor $r_M$ and the abatement coefficient $g$ applied to displacement-controlled moments in type-P assessment against immediate excessive deformation and plastic instability.

The code methodology is compared with elastic and elasto-plastic analyses of a stainless-steel piping configuration with an elbow subjected to restrained thermal expansion. A beam-based piping model, using a bend flexibility factor, is first compared with a shell finite-element model; the elastic anchor moments agree closely when the flexibility factor is included. For the investigated case, the RCC-MRx coefficient is approximately $0.323$, while a reaction-moment-based effective factor from the elasto-plastic model is approximately $0.436$. These values are obtained by different procedures and their comparison alone does not establish whether the code rule is conservative. The study is limited to monotonic loading without pressure or creep.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:Intro}
% =============================================================================

Steel pipings are metallic structures (usually pressurized) consisting of straight tubes with hollow cicular cross-sections, assembled by means of welds, fittings (elbows, reducers, tees, etc.) or flanged connections. Due to functional and safety requirements, components such as valves, flow meters, expansion joints and other in-line components might be assembled on piping tubes. The entire structure can be connected to pressurized equipments by means of nozzles and supported by dedicated support structures. Together, these elements constitute a large and complex structure that must be addressed through rigorous engineering analysis.\\

\subsection{One-dimensional piping models}

Piping systems may extend over kilometres, making a detailed three-dimensional finite element analysis of the entire system costly to prepare, solve and post-process. Piping stress analysis therefore commonly represents the system by a one-dimensional model of its centreline, with the pipe sections and components described by their mechanical properties.

In the Timoshenko beam formulation used in this work, a two-node spatial element has six degrees of freedom at each node: three translations and three rotations, for a total of twelve. In addition to axial deformation, bending and torsion, Timoshenko beam theory accounts for transverse shear deformation. This model provides an efficient way to calculate displacements and sectional forces and moments throughout the piping system.

A conventional beam element does not explicitly represent deformation of the pipe cross-section. This limitation is particularly important for bends: under bending, the initially circular section tends to ovalise, making the bend more flexible than a model that retains an undeformed cross-section. Figure~\ref{fig:ovalisation} illustrates the effect in a shell model. To account for this additional flexibility in beam-based piping analysis, design codes provide bend flexibility factors.

Early work by von K\'arm\'an and Hovgaard addressed bend flexibility, and simplified approximations were subsequently developed by Beskin. For the smooth, unpressurised elbow considered in the benchmark, the flexibility factor $k$ is expressed in terms of the non-dimensional flexibility characteristic $h$ as
\begin{equation}
h = \frac{t\,R}{r^{2}}, \qquad k = \frac{1.65}{h},
\label{eq:flexfactor}
\end{equation}
where $t$ is the wall thickness, $r$ the mean cross-sectional radius, and $R$ the bend radius. The factor modifies the bending flexibility used in the beam model. The pressure correction is not included in this expression.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figures/elbow_ovalisation_contour2.png}
\caption{Finite-element illustration of elbow cross-section ovalisation under in-plane bending: the stress concentration follows the ovalised region around the crown of the bend.}
\label{fig:ovalisation}
\end{figure}

Flexibility factors address the overall deformation of bends, whereas stress intensification factors are used in piping-code stress calculations to account for the increased stress associated with components such as bends and fittings, following work such as Markl's experimental studies \cite{Markl1955}.



\subsection{Linear elastic analysis}

The one-dimensional model describes the geometry and structural degrees of freedom; a material law is also required to calculate its response. Standard piping calculations generally assume linear elastic material behaviour, using Hooke's law to obtain displacements and sectional forces and moments under the specified loads. Stresses are then evaluated from the calculated loads and the relevant piping-code expressions.

Hooke's law is retained for the elastic strain component throughout this work: in uniaxial notation, $\sigma=E\varepsilon_e$. When plasticity is included, the total strain is $\varepsilon_t=\varepsilon_e+\varepsilon_p$, so $\sigma=E\varepsilon_t$ no longer applies; the plastic component is modelled separately by the Ramberg--Osgood law introduced in Section~\ref{sec:RO}.

The resulting elastic stresses cannot, by themselves, establish the actual response or the risk of damage once local yielding occurs. They must be interpreted in relation to the nature of the loading and the failure modes addressed by the applicable code. This motivates the stress classification discussed below.

\subsection{Interpretation: primary and secondary stresses}
\label{sec:standardApproach}

Piping design codes distinguish between \emph{primary} and \emph{secondary} stresses. Primary stresses are associated with the equilibrium of applied loads, such as pressure or weight. Secondary stresses arise from compatibility constraints, for example those associated with restrained thermal expansion, and are commonly described as self-limiting. \\
Figure~\ref{fig:P_S} shows an example of the two stresses.  \textbf{E} presents the stress state of a material point predicted by an elastic approach.  \textbf{P} and \textbf{S} are stress states responding respectively to primary and secondary loads, considering the elastoplastic law.\\
For the idealised uniaxial, force-controlled case shown in Figure~\ref{fig:P_S}, equilibrium requires $\sigma_P=\sigma_E$, while plasticity may cause the strain $\varepsilon_P$ to exceed the elastic prediction $\varepsilon_E$. In a structure, load-controlled stresses can contribute to excessive deformation and plastic collapse, as well as elastic or elastoplastic instability. The equality shown in the figure illustrates this simple case and is not a general statement that local stresses cannot redistribute after yielding.

For the idealised displacement-controlled case in Figure~\ref{fig:P_S}, the total strain is prescribed, so that $\varepsilon_S=\varepsilon_E$. Plasticity can then reduce the stress below the elastic prediction: $\sigma_S\leq\sigma_E$. Restrained thermal expansion, anchor movements and differential settlement can generate secondary stresses; their cyclic variation may contribute to fatigue or progressive deformation. In an actual piping system, however, an imposed displacement at the boundary does not necessarily prescribe the strain at each section: elastic follow-up can allow local plastic strains to increase, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:EFF}.

Piping design codes use these categories and the associated failure modes to define assessment criteria for results obtained from elastic calculations. This provides a practical alternative to detailed nonlinear analysis within the scope of the applicable rules.

\section{Elastic follow-up}
% =============================================================================
As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:Intro}, piping design rules use elastic calculations and stress classification to assess structural behaviour. When inelastic deformation is concentrated in part of a piping system, the surrounding structure can continue to act as a spring on that region. This interaction, known as \emph{elastic follow-up}, makes the local response dependent on the behaviour of the system as a whole \cite{RCCMRx,ASMEIII5}.

The present paper considers monotonic plasticity in the negligible-creep regime. Roche's report on elevated-temperature piping \cite{Roche1985} provides important background on the interaction between local and global behaviour, but addresses creep relaxation; its results are therefore distinguished here from the monotonic RCC-MRx rules. In RB~3643.31 and RB~3651.113, RCC-MRx provides a practical route to account for elastic follow-up and the effect of plasticity on displacement-controlled moments.

The monotonic plastic response is represented here by a Ramberg--Osgood law, specified first below before its use in the physical illustration and in the subsequent code assessment.

\subsection{Monotonic material behaviour}
\label{sec:RO}

To model the elasto-plastic material response, the Ramberg--Osgood law is used in its general textbook form, decomposing the total strain into an elastic and a power-law plastic part,
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon_t = \varepsilon_e + \varepsilon_p = \frac{\sigma}{E} + \left(\frac{\sigma}{K}\right)^{1/n},
\label{eq:RO-generic}
\end{equation}
with $K$ a strength coefficient and $n$ a hardening exponent. In the benchmark, the mean monotonic tensile curve is expressed using the temperature-dependent stress scale
\begin{equation}
K_R(T) = C_0\,R_{p0.2,\mathrm{mean}}(T),
\label{eq:KofT}
\end{equation}
where $R_{p0.2,\mathrm{mean}}$ is the mean 0.2\% proof stress. With strains expressed as dimensionless fractions rather than percentages, the form used in the benchmark is
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon(\sigma) = \frac{\sigma}{E} + 0.01\left(\frac{\sigma}{C_0\,R_{p0.2,\mathrm{mean}}}\right)^{1/n_0},
\label{eq:RO}
\end{equation}
The factor $0.01$ converts the plastic-strain term from percent to fractional strain. Accordingly, $K_R$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:KofT} is not identical to $K$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:RO-generic}; the two forms coincide for $n=n_0$ when $K=K_R/0.01^{n_0}$. The curve is uniaxial; its application to the reference stress in the code method is described below.

Figure~\ref{fig:RO-n0-parametric} compares the benchmark curve with lower values of $n_0$. For each value, $C_0=0.2^{-n_0}$ is recalibrated so that the mean 0.2\% proof stress remains fixed at \SI{106}{MPa}. With this recalibration, decreasing $n_0$ reduces strain hardening (work hardening): the post-yield stress increase for a given increase in plastic strain progressively diminishes and tends to zero as $n_0\to0^+$. The dashed line shows the resulting ideal elastic--perfectly plastic limit; setting $n_0=0$ directly in Eq.~\eqref{eq:RO} is not defined.

\subsection{Elastic follow-up: physical interpretation}
\label{sec:EFF}

Bar 1 is subjected to a primary load, while Bar 2 is subjected to a secondary load. Bar 3 is identical to the other two bars but is connected to an additional spring and is also subjected to a secondary load. In his conclusions, Roland L. Roche showed that Bar 3, although subjected only to a secondary load, exhibits an intermediate behavior between those associated with purely primary and purely secondary loading. With our own selected beam geometry and material, the test is reproduced and shown in figure \ref{fig:3beams}.

The applied forces and imposed displacements for the three beams were selected such that, under a purely elastic analysis, all three beams exhibit the same stress and strain state \textbf{E}: $ \varepsilon_E=0.081\%$ and $\sigma_E = 126\,\mathrm{MPa}$. \\

When the Ramberg--Osgood plasticity law described in Section~\ref{sec:RO} is introduced into the common section, beam 1 (point \textbf{P}) exhibits a purely primary stress response, as expected: $ \varepsilon_P=1\% >  \varepsilon_E $ and $\sigma_P = 126\,\mathrm{MPa}=\sigma_E$. Similarly, beam 2 (pont \textbf{S}) exhibits a purely secondary stress response: $ \varepsilon_S=0.081\% =  \varepsilon_E $ and $\sigma_S = 82.3\,\mathrm{MPa} < \sigma_E$. This distinction between primary and secondary load responses was previously illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:P_S} and is reproduced here in Figure~\ref{fig:deviation}. However, beam 3 (point \textbf{SP}), \emph{although subjected to a secondary load}, exhibits an intermediate stress state between the purely secondary and purely primary configurations. Point SP lies between points S and P on the elasto-plastic stress--strain curve: $ \varepsilon_S<  \left( \varepsilon_{PS}=0.16\% \right) < \varepsilon_P $ and  $\sigma_S < \left( \sigma_{SP}= 95.85\,\mathrm{MPa} \right) < \sigma_P $. \\

Beam 3 in the above example might be understood as a symbolic representation of a piping structure. The common section acts as the source of plasticity due to stress concentrations occurring at specific weak points within the structure. In reality, these vulnerable locations are not only subjected to direct local loading, but are also influenced by loads transfer through the surrounding parts of the piping system. These less vulnerable regions experience lower stress levels and therefore remain entirely within the elastic regime. In this context, the behavior of the weak links responding to the imposed-secondary loads might be deviated compared to the case when no intermediate structure is present, based on how the intermediate structure (spring) elastically responds to the secondary loads. \\

In the example shown in figure \ref{fig:deviation}, the spring section has a relatively small stiffness compared with the one of the common section, i.e., $k_{spring}=\frac{1}{4}k_{section}$. This stiffness $k_{spring}$ might be viewed as representing a long adjacent piping segment containing several loops, which significantly reduces its overall stiffness. It is also worth noting that the spring section imposes a boundary condition for the common section. Thanks to the surrounding pipings' flexibility, plastic deformation is more likely to develop in the weak zone (the common section). As a result, the position of point \textbf{SP} deviates from its initially predicted location (point \textbf{S}) under the applied secondary load. The displacement remains imposed at the boundary, but the deformation of the weak section is not fixed independently of the surrounding spring. \\
Moreover, if the spring stiffness is almost zero, plastic deformation can develop almost freely in the weak zone. As a result, point \textbf{SP} converges toward the position of point \textbf{P} (the purely primary-load case), and Beam~3 exhibits essentially the same response as Beam~1. Conversely, if the surrounding structure is very stiff (with an almost infinite stiffness), the weak zone is strongly constrained and plasticity develops in a more controlled manner. In this case, point \textbf{SP} approaches the position of point \textbf{S} (the purely secondary-load case) on the elastoplastic curve, and Beam~3 exhibits essentially the same response as Beam~2. \\

The above remarks are illustrated by points B and D in Figure~\ref{fig:parametric-EFU}. For point B, the spring stiffness is increased to \(k/2\), where \(k\) is the stiffness of the common section. The imposed displacement is increased to 2.435\,mm to achieve the same elastic solution as that predicted for point E. As expected, compared with point SP, the higher spring stiffness causes point B to move closer to the secondary-behavior limit (point S), yielding \(\sigma_B = 90.3\,\text{MPa}\) and \(\varepsilon_B = 0.13\%\). Similarly, for point D, the initial spring stiffness is reduced to \(k/16\), and the imposed displacement is adjusted to 27.591\,mm. As expected, point D moves closer to the primary-behavior limit (point P), yielding \(\sigma_D = 106.7\,\text{MPa}\) and \(\varepsilon_D = 0.283\%\). This parametric study also shows that purely primary behavior is approached as the spring stiffness tends to zero, whereas purely secondary behavior is approached in the limit of infinitely stiff springs.

\subsection{Elastic follow-up factor $r_M$ in RCC-MRx}
\label{sec:rccmrx}
% =============================================================================

The RCC-MRx monotonic elastic follow-up factor relates the local response to the behaviour of the whole piping system. The simplified derivations and their assumptions are retained for review in Appendix~\ref{app:derivation-overview}.

RB~3643.31 characterises the monotonic elastic follow-up factor at a section using the elastic state $(\sigma_{\mathrm{el}},\varepsilon_{\mathrm{el}})$ and the corresponding monotonic stress and strain $(\sigma,\varepsilon)$:
\begin{equation}
r_M \;=\; -\frac{E(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{el}})}{\sigma-\sigma_{\mathrm{el}}},
\label{eq:rM}
\end{equation}
with $r_M=0$ for the purely secondary limiting case and $r_M\to\infty$ for the purely primary limiting case. The code permits any justified assessment method; its default method evaluates $r_M=T/t-1$, with $r_M\geq0$. Here $t$ is the \emph{local} resilience and $T$ a \emph{global} resilience weighted over the piping system:
\begin{equation}
T = \frac{\int \sigma_{\mathrm{ref}}^2 A\, ds}{\displaystyle\int \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{ref}}^2 A}{t}\, ds}, \qquad
\frac{1}{t} = \frac{\varepsilon_{M,p}(\sigma_{\mathrm{ref}})}{\sigma_{\mathrm{ref}}/E}, \qquad
\sigma_{\mathrm{ref}}^2 = \left[0.79\,B_2\,\frac{(m)'_R}{Z}\right]^2 + \left[0.87\,\frac{m_1}{Z}\right]^2,
\label{eq:Tglobal}
\end{equation}
Here $\varepsilon_{M,p}$ is the plastic part of the monotonic tensile curve, $A$ is the cross-sectional area, $s$ the curvilinear abscissa along the system, and $Z$ the section modulus. Both moments arise from imposed displacements: $(m)'_R$ is the resultant bending moment and $m_1$ the torsional moment. The stress factor $B_2$ is given by RB~3680; for tees and branch connections the code specifies $B_2=1$ in this calculation.

Figure~\ref{fig:roche-graphical} illustrates this construction for a uniaxial reference stress $\sigma_0=\sigma_{\mathrm{ref}}$ and its elastic strain $\varepsilon_0=\sigma_0/E$. The horizontal segments give $t=OM/MN$, while the default value $r_M=T/t-1$ determines the slope of the elastic-follow-up line. Its intersection with the monotonic curve represents the corresponding elasto-plastic state. Writing $\varphi=\sigma/\sigma_0$, $\varepsilon_0^p=\varepsilon_{M,p}(\sigma_0)>0$ and $\Phi(\varphi)=\varepsilon_{M,p}(\varphi\sigma_0)/\varepsilon_0^p$, the strain at that point is $\varepsilon=\varphi\varepsilon_0+\Phi(\varphi)\varepsilon_0^p$. This schematic interpretation is not a general derivation of the default method.

The reference stress is a scalar used with the uniaxial material curve, not necessarily a local peak von Mises stress. Roche connects $\pi B_2/4$ to reference-stress and limit-moment considerations and discusses the combination of bending and torsion \cite{Roche1985}.

For a very thin annulus, Roark's \emph{Formulas for Stress and Strain} \cite[Table~A.1, case~17, p.~807]{YoungBudynas2002} gives the bending shape factor $Z_{\mathrm{pl}}/Z_{\mathrm{el}}=4/\pi$. Its reciprocal therefore gives the coefficient in the limit-load reference stress:
\begin{equation*}
\sigma_{\mathrm{ref}}
=\frac{M_b}{Z_{\mathrm{pl}}}
=\frac{Z_{\mathrm{el}}}{Z_{\mathrm{pl}}}\frac{M_b}{Z_{\mathrm{el}}}
\simeq\frac{\pi}{4}\frac{M_b}{Z_{\mathrm{el}}}.
\end{equation*}
Thus $\pi/4\simeq0.785$ explains the coefficient $0.79$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:Tglobal} for thin-tube bending. Here $Z_{\mathrm{el}}$ is the elastic section modulus denoted by $Z$ in that equation; Roark's table instead uses $Z$ for the plastic section modulus. This section-property argument does not by itself establish the component factor $B_2$ or the full rule for elbows.

For a circular tube, $0.87\approx\sqrt{3}/2$ is consistent with the von Mises conversion of the torsional shear stress. These observations illuminate the code coefficients; they do not establish the full historical derivation of the current rule. The separate factor $2(1+\nu)/3$, which appears in some equivalent-strain definitions, has a different origin (Section~\ref{sec:equivalent_sigma_epsilon_eq}).

\subsection{Reduction of displacement-controlled moments: coefficient $g$}
Up to this point, we have reviewed how standard piping codes address complex structural behavior through linear analysis supplemented by stress categorization (§\ref{sec:standardApproach}), examined the limitations of the secondary stress concept in the presence of elastic follow-up (§\ref{sec:EFF}), and presented the quantification of elastic follow-up through the RCC-MRx methodology (§\ref{sec:rccmrx}).


This observation raises an important question, particularly in light of the results presented in Figure \ref{fig:deviation}. Due to the presence of elastic follow-up, loads traditionally classified as secondary exhibit a deviation from the expected secondary-stress behavior and partially resemble the behavior of primary loads. This observation highlights the need to account for the contribution of secondary loads when assessing the acceptability of primary stresses.


The RCC-MRx RB~3651.1131 type-P \(s_m+s_b\) assessment is intended to prevent excessive deformation and plastic instability. It checks the combined stress against the relatively low allowable \(S^*\), rather than using a shakedown limit that may permit stresses approaching twice the yield strength. The Code nevertheless credits the reduction in displacement-controlled moment that plasticity can produce: the elastic-analysis moment \(m\) is multiplied by \(g\), which accounts for plasticity and elastic follow-up, while the load-controlled moment \(M\) remains unattenuated. Thus, the bending term is \(M+g\,m\) before the combined stress is checked against \(S^*\). The graphical interpretation of \(g\) is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:rccmrx-official}. RB~3651.1132 defines the coefficient as
\begin{equation} 
g \;=\; \frac{(\sigma-\sigma_P)(1+r_M)} {E\left[\varepsilon_{M,p}(\sigma)-\varepsilon_{M,p}(\sigma_P)\right] + (\sigma-\sigma_P)(1+r_M)} . 
\label{eq:gfull}
\end{equation} 
Here, \(\varepsilon_{M,p}\) denotes the plastic strain on the mean monotonic tensile curve, represented here by the Ramberg--Osgood relation. The code defines the primary pressure stress as \(\sigma_P = 0.87\,\dfrac{P D_i}{2h_c}\), which must be lower than \(\sigma\). Equation~\eqref{eq:gfull} uses both the stress difference \(\sigma-\sigma_P\) and the corresponding difference in plastic strain.

The coefficient \(0.87\) is consistent with the von Mises equivalent stress for a thin-walled, closed-end pipe under internal pressure. Neglecting radial stress, the hoop and axial membrane stresses are approximately \(\sigma_\theta=PD_i/(2h_c)\) and \(\sigma_z=\sigma_\theta/2\), respectively. Thus,
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\mathrm{VM}}=\sqrt{\sigma_\theta^2+\sigma_z^2-\sigma_\theta\sigma_z}
=\frac{\sqrt{3}}{2}\,\sigma_\theta
\simeq 0.87\,\frac{P D_i}{2h_c}.
\end{equation}
This idealised stress state explains the numerical factor but does not establish its historical derivation in the code; other boundary conditions or significant radial stresses can alter the equivalent stress.

For this calculation, RB~3651.1132 specifies the maximum monotonic elastic follow-up factor of the piping line.

\paragraph{Derivation from the graphical construction.}
Equation~\eqref{eq:gfull} follows directly from the two prescribed slopes in Figure~\ref{fig:rccmrx-official}. Let $A=(\varepsilon_A,\sigma_P)$ and $B=(\varepsilon_B,\sigma)$ be the two points on the mean monotonic tensile curve. Let $C=(\varepsilon_C,\sigma_C)$ be the intersection of the line through $A$ of slope $E$ with the line through $B$ of slope $-E/r_M$. The upper stress level shown in the figure is
\begin{equation*}
\sigma_C=\sigma_P+\frac{\sigma-\sigma_P}{g},
\qquad\text{hence}\qquad
g=\frac{\sigma-\sigma_P}{\sigma_C-\sigma_P}.
\end{equation*}
The line $AC$ has slope $E$, while the slope of $CB$ gives, for distinct points $B$ and $C$,
\begin{equation*}
r_M=-\frac{E(\varepsilon_B-\varepsilon_C)}{\sigma-\sigma_C},
\qquad
1+r_M=
\frac{-E(\varepsilon_B-\varepsilon_C)+\sigma-\sigma_C}
{\sigma-\sigma_C}.
\end{equation*}
The strain at $B$ follows the mean tensile curve. Because $AC$ starts at $A$, the strain at $C$ includes the plastic strain already present at $\sigma_P$:
\begin{equation*}
\varepsilon_B=\frac{\sigma}{E}+\varepsilon_{M,p}(\sigma),
\qquad
\varepsilon_C=\frac{\sigma_C}{E}+\varepsilon_{M,p}(\sigma_P).
\end{equation*}
Substituting these strains and cancelling the elastic terms yields
\begin{align*}
1+r_M
&=\frac{-\sigma-E\varepsilon_{M,p}(\sigma)+\sigma_C
+E\varepsilon_{M,p}(\sigma_P)+\sigma-\sigma_C}{\sigma-\sigma_C}\\
&=\frac{E[\varepsilon_{M,p}(\sigma)-\varepsilon_{M,p}(\sigma_P)]}
{\sigma_C-\sigma}.
\end{align*}
Multiplying the numerator and denominator of the stress-increment ratio by $1+r_M$, and separating $\sigma_C-\sigma_P$ into $(\sigma_C-\sigma)+(\sigma-\sigma_P)$, gives
\begin{align*}
g
&=\frac{\sigma-\sigma_P}{\sigma_C-\sigma_P}
=\frac{(\sigma-\sigma_P)(1+r_M)}
{(\sigma_C-\sigma)(1+r_M)+(\sigma-\sigma_P)(1+r_M)}\\
&=\frac{(\sigma-\sigma_P)(1+r_M)}
{E[\varepsilon_{M,p}(\sigma)-\varepsilon_{M,p}(\sigma_P)]
+(\sigma-\sigma_P)(1+r_M)},
\end{align*}
which is Eq.~\eqref{eq:gfull}. The limiting cases where $B$ and $C$ coincide, including the $r_M=0$ case with $CB$ vertical, are recovered by continuity. The term $\varepsilon_{M,p}(\sigma_P)$ appears because $AC$ starts at $A$ on the material curve, not at the undeformed origin. Thus $\sigma_C$ is an auxiliary stress of the graphical construction, not automatically the original elastic-analysis stress. This derivation establishes the equivalence of the Code's scalar graphical and algebraic constructions; it does not independently derive the prescribed maximum line factor or the multiaxial piping assessment. Without pressure, $\sigma_P=0$ and $\varepsilon_{M,p}(0)=0$, point $A$ is the origin and $g=\sigma/\sigma_C$.

The code evaluates \(g\) at a prescribed stress level rather than requiring an elastoplastic analysis. RB~3651.1132 specifies:
\begin{equation*} 
\sigma = \min\left(2\,S^{*}\,\frac{(R_{p0.2})_{moy}}{(R_{p0.2})_{\min}},\, (R_m)_{\min}\right)
\end{equation*}
with
\begin{equation*} 
S^{*} = \alpha \left[ 0.426\,(R_{p0.2})_{\min}(\theta_m) + 0.032\,(R_m)_{\min}(\theta_m) \right]
\end{equation*}
The ratio of mean to minimum proof stress is taken as 1.25 unless another value is justified by the designer. The code also requires $\sigma_P<\sigma$ and specifies the applicable temperature and material-curve conditions.

\section{Case study: elastic and elasto-plastic piping analysis}
% =============================================================================
This case study considers a simple piping configuration consisting of two straight pipe legs, anchored at both ends and connected by an elbow, under restrained thermal expansion only. The analysis is deliberately staged to separate structural-model calibration from material plasticity. First, a linear-elastic benchmark compares a one-dimensional beam model with a three-dimensional shell finite-element model discretised using four-node bilinear thick-shell elements (MSC Marc Element 75); the elbow flexibility is calibrated so that the two models provide a consistent elastic reference. In the stress--strain schematic of Figure~\ref{fig:P_S}, this elastic calculation corresponds to state E. Plasticity is then introduced in the shell model while retaining the benchmark geometry, boundary conditions and thermal loading, and replacing the linear-elastic constitutive law with a nonlinear material law. This elasto-plastic calculation is used to assess the response of the same piping configuration, not to reproduce Roland Roche's method.

The configuration consists of two straight pipe segments (10-20 and 30-40) connected by an elbow (20-30). The structure is classified in RCC-MRx as material group A3.3S (2022 Edition), the group for solution-annealed austenitic stainless steels in the 316L family, including X2CrNiMo17-12-2, X2CrNiMo17-12-3 and X2CrNiMo18-14-3 (Section III, Tome 1, Subsection Z, Appendix A3.3S). The nonlinear constitutive curve used below is specifically parameterized from 316L(N) tensile data at \SI{550}{\celsius}; thus A3.3S is the code property-group designation, while 316L(N) identifies the material dataset used in the analysis. The pipe has an outer diameter of 219.07 mm, a wall thickness of 3.76 mm, a straight-pipe length of 2000 mm for each segment, and an elbow radius of 500 mm. The straight-pipe ends at nodes 10 and 40 are fully anchored. The piping is uniformly heated from an ambient temperature of 20°C to an in-service temperature of 550°C. Only thermal expansion is considered in this analysis, and the material behavior is assumed to be linear elastic. At \SI{550}{\celsius}, the elastic material properties are $E=\SI{155}{GPa}$ and $\nu=0.3$; the thermal expansion coefficient used is $\alpha=\SI{18.5e-6}{\per\celsius}$. \\

Two independent numerical models are used and cross-checked:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Aspect Nuclear Pipe Stress} (``NS'' solver, PEPS~8.0), a Timoshenko-beam FEA piping model in which the elbow flexibility is represented through the analytical flexibility factor $k$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:flexfactor};
    \item \textbf{MSC~Marc}, a shell finite-element model using bilinear thick-shell elements (four-node, Marc Element~75), in which elbow ovalisation is captured directly by the shell discretisation rather than through an analytical flexibility factor. This element uses bilinear interpolation of coordinates, displacements and rotations, with membrane strains from the displacement field and curvatures from the rotation field; transverse shear strains are evaluated at the mid-edges and interpolated to the Gauss integration points, giving an efficient element that remains accurate in the thin-shell limit while avoiding tying constraints at plate intersections. The two ends of the model are connected to the FE mesh through RBE2 rigid-body elements, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:shell-mesh-views}. RBE2 links can locally generate spurious over-stiffening and stress concentrations; this is considered acceptable here because stresses are not examined at the anchor points themselves (only the elbow region is of interest for stress results), so that the simpler RBE2 connection is preferred over an RBE3 connection.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Elasto-plastic analysis}

Section~\ref{sec:RO} introduces the Ramberg--Osgood law used here: $n_0$ controls the power-law exponent $1/n_0$ and hence the normalised curve shape, while $C_0$ scales the stress through $K_R=C_0R_{p0.2,\mathrm{mean}}$. For 316L(N), the reference values are $n_0=0.1125$ ($1/n_0=8.89$) and $C_0=1.198$. At the benchmark temperature of \SI{550}{\celsius}, $R_{p0.2}\approx\SI{106}{MPa}$, giving a stress scale $C_0R_{p0.2}\approx\SI{127}{MPa}$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:RO}. The temperature dependence enters through $R_{p0.2}(T)$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:KofT}; $n_0$ and $C_0$ are held fixed, so no change in normalised hardening-curve shape with temperature is assumed. A separate sensitivity study at \SI{500}{\celsius} (where $R_{p0.2}\approx\SI{110}{MPa}$, so that the reference parameter set gives $C_0R_{p0.2}\approx\SI{131.8}{MPa}$) compares this reference curve with an alternative fit ($C_0=1.233$, $n_0=0.130$, i.e. $1/n_0=7.69$, $C_0R_{p0.2}\approx\SI{135.6}{MPa}$) calibrated on tensile data at the same temperature; the resulting stress--strain curve is only marginally softer than the reference one over the whole strain range plotted, confirming that the choice of hardening parameters is not the dominant source of discrepancy discussed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.



The elasto-plastic shell analysis uses the von Mises yield criterion with isotropic hardening, and the Ramberg--Osgood law of Eq.~\eqref{eq:RO} calibrated to 316L(N) at \SI{550}{\celsius} (RCC-MRx RB~A3.3S.451), with yielding onset defined at zero offset stress consistent with the code's convention. The material is defined in MSC~Marc by $E=\SI{155000}{MPa}$, $\nu=0.3$, and the von Mises yield condition $\sigma_{\mathrm{VM}} < \sigma_Y$ with
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\mathrm{VM}} := \sqrt{\tfrac32\,s_{ij}s_{ij}} ,
\end{equation}
$s_{ij}$ the deviatoric stress. Hardening is tracked through the equivalent plastic strain
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon_{p,\mathrm{eq}} \;:=\; \sqrt{\tfrac{2}{3}\,\varepsilon^{p}_{ij}\varepsilon^{p}_{ij}} \, ,
\label{eq:eps-p-eq}
\end{equation}
with $\varepsilon^{p}_{ij}$ the plastic strain tensor, consistent with the standard von Mises formulation. Isotropic hardening is imposed as a tabulated function $\sigma_Y(\varepsilon_{p,\mathrm{eq}}) := F(V1)$, with $V1$ the independent (equivalent plastic strain) variable digitised from the Ramberg--Osgood law of Eq.~\eqref{eq:RO} at zero offset stress. The \emph{plas\_new} table samples this curve over 34 points, $V1\in[0,1.002]$, $F\in[0.6,213.2]\,\mathrm{MPa}$ (dimensionless scale factor $\times 100$), as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:hardening-plas-new}.

\subsection{Observations and discussions}
\label{sec:discussion}

The RCC-MRx elastic route (RB~3651.1131) reduces the secondary (displacement-dependent) moment $m$ by the coefficient $g$ and combines the abated stress with the primary stress in the $\sigma_m+\sigma_b$ (P-type) damage check. For a fixed linear moment-to-stress conversion, multiplying the moment by $g$ also multiplies its associated stress contribution by $g$; this does not imply the same reduction of the local von Mises stress after plastic redistribution. The natural benchmark question is then: \emph{how does the RCC-MRx-derived $g$ (and the associated $\sigma_m+\sigma_b$) compare with the moment reduction and stress obtained from the elasto-plastic FE reference calculation?}

To define the FE comparison quantity before introducing its numerical value, the benchmark presentation reports the shell-model reaction-moment magnitudes as $M_{\mathrm{reaction,el}}=\SI{40.3}{\kilo\newton\metre}$ for the elastic calculation (the rounded \SI{40.29}{\kilo\newton\metre} result of Section~\ref{sec:elastic-analysis}) and $M_{\mathrm{reaction,ep}}=\SI{17.58}{\kilo\newton\metre}$ for the elasto-plastic calculation under the same thermal loading. Their ratio defines a reaction-based effective abatement coefficient:
\begin{equation}
g_{\mathrm{reaction}}
:=\frac{M_{\mathrm{reaction,ep}}}{M_{\mathrm{reaction,el}}}
=\frac{17.58}{40.3}
\simeq 0.436.
\label{eq:g-reaction-fe}
\end{equation}
Thus, $0.436$ is obtained directly from the two FE reaction moments, not from the RCC-MRx formula for $g$. It measures the reduction of the reported global reaction moment, rather than a local stress reduction. It is retained as a moment-based comparator because the code coefficient acts on displacement-dependent moments; identifying it with the local code coefficient remains a modelling approximation.

The stress-based comparison uses the elastic maximum $\sigma_{\mathrm{VM,el}}=\SI{213.7}{MPa}$ documented in Figure~\ref{fig:elastic-vonmises} and the elasto-plastic maximum $\sigma_{\mathrm{VM,ep}}=\SI{86.5}{MPa}$ shown in Figure~\ref{fig:vonmises}, giving a different ratio:
\begin{equation}
g_{\mathrm{VM}}
:=\frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{VM,ep}}}{\sigma_{\mathrm{VM,el}}}
=\frac{86.5}{213.7}
\simeq 0.405.
\label{eq:g-vm-fe}
\end{equation}
The two ratios need not coincide because plastic redistribution changes the local stress field as well as the global reaction. Table~\ref{tab:g-comparison} reports both $g_{\mathrm{reaction}}$ and $g_{\mathrm{VM}}$, alongside the same elasto-plastic von Mises stress: \SI{86.5}{MPa} is a direct FE result, not the product $0.436\times\SI{213.7}{MPa}$.

The following scalar reconstruction is compared with the directly extracted reaction ratio of Eq.~\eqref{eq:g-reaction-fe}; substituting its compatible stress back into the code expression is an algebraic consistency check, not an independent FE determination of $g$.

The shell-model reaction moment at the critical section is $M_f=\SI{10.0927}{\kilo\newton\metre}$. Using the piping-code bending coefficient $B_2=4.371$ and section modulus $Z=\SI{134592}{\milli\metre^3}$ gives the equivalent elastic reference stress
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{0,\mathrm{ref}} = 0.79\,B_2\,\frac{M_f}{Z}
= \SI{258.938}{MPa}.
\label{eq:fe-reference-stress}
\end{equation}
The corresponding elasto-plastic stress is obtained by solving the elastic-follow-up compatibility relation together with the Ramberg--Osgood law of Eq.~\eqref{eq:RO}:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\sigma_{0,\mathrm{ref}}/E-\varepsilon(\sigma)}
{\sigma_{0,\mathrm{ref}}-\sigma}
= -\frac{r_M}{E},
\qquad
\varepsilon(\sigma)=\frac{\sigma}{E}
+0.01\left(\frac{\sigma}{C_0R_{p0.2,\mathrm{mean}}}\right)^{1/n_0}.
\end{equation}
With $E=\SI{155}{GPa}$, $r_M=2.638$, $C_0=1.198$, $n_0=0.1125$, and $R_{p0.2,\mathrm{mean}}=\SI{106}{MPa}$, this gives $\sigma_{3,\mathrm{est}}=\SI{112.6}{MPa}$. For the benchmark, $\sigma_P=0$; hence Eq.~\eqref{eq:gfull} reduces to
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
\varepsilon_{M,p}(\sigma_{3,\mathrm{est}})
&=0.01\left(
\frac{\sigma_{3,\mathrm{est}}}{C_0R_{p0.2,\mathrm{mean}}}
\right)^{1/n_0}
= 3.434\times10^{-3},\\
g
&=\frac{\sigma_{3,\mathrm{est}}(1+r_M)}
{E\,\varepsilon_{M,p}(\sigma_{3,\mathrm{est}})
+\sigma_{3,\mathrm{est}}(1+r_M)}
=0.435.
\end{aligned}
\label{eq:fe-reconstructed-g}
\end{equation}
This scalar reconstruction is numerically close to the direct reaction-moment ratio $g_{\mathrm{reaction}}=0.436$, but the two quantities are obtained by different procedures.

Two observations follow directly from Table~\ref{tab:g-comparison}:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Observation 1:} the RCC-MRx abatement coefficient ($g=0.323$) is less conservative than the coefficient implied by the elasto-plastic FE reference ($g=0.436$).
\item \textbf{Observation 2:} the RCC-MRx primary-equivalent stress ($\sigma_m+\sigma_b = \SI{66.5}{MPa}$) is less conservative than the von Mises stress of the reference elasto-plastic FE analysis (\SI{86.5}{MPa}).
\end{enumerate}

\underline{\textbf{Discussion: why is the RCC-MRx route less conservative?}}


RCC-MRx clause RB~3651.111 sets the level-A limits for primary stresses due to pressure:
\[
P_m \le S_m(\theta_m), \qquad
P_L \le 1.5\,S_m(\theta_m), \qquad
P_L + P_b \le 1.5\,S_m(\theta_m).
\]
Within that same elastic-analysis framework, the subsequent conversion argument then moves to RB~3651.112, i.e.\ the general primary membrane-plus-bending check used here. RCC-MRx clause RB~3651.112 requires that, for load combinations governed by level-A criteria, the general primary membrane-plus-bending stress additionally satisfies
\begin{equation}
\overline{p_m+p_b} = B_1\,P\left[\frac{D_e}{2h_c}\right] + \left\langle B_2/Z,\,M\right\rangle \le 1.3\,S_m(\theta_m),
\label{eq:pmpb}
\end{equation}
i.e. the abated, displacement-dependent stress must ultimately be re-expressed and re-checked on the strictly primary allowable basis $1.3\,S_m(\theta_m)$, rather than on the CMS1 allowable $S^{*}$ used to derive $g$ in Section~\ref{sec:rccmrx}. For the benchmark section considered here, $1.3\,S_m = \SI{100.1}{MPa}$ while $S^{*} = \SI{48.156}{MPa}$, so that the ratio
\begin{equation}
\frac{1.3\,S_m}{S^{*}} = \frac{100.1}{48.156} = 2.079
\end{equation}
is the conversion factor that must be applied when re-scaling the RCC-MRx abatement coefficient and stress from the $S^{*}$ (CMS1) basis onto the $1.3\,S_m$ (primary) basis:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
g_{\mathrm{RCC\text{-}MRx}}^{\mathrm{rabat}} &=
g\cdot\frac{1.3\,S_m}{S^{*}}
= 0.323\times 2.079
= 0.671, \\
(\sigma_m+\sigma_b)^{\mathrm{rabat}} &=
(\sigma_m+\sigma_b)\cdot\frac{1.3\,S_m}{S^{*}}
= \SI{138.23}{MPa}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\textcolor{red}{Let's review the above paragraph to see if it's appropriate to keep it in the article} \\



Once this conversion is applied, the RCC-MRx-based primary-equivalent stress ($g^{\mathrm{rabat}}=0.671$, $(\sigma_m+\sigma_b)^{\mathrm{rabat}}=\SI{138.23}{MPa}$) becomes markedly \emph{more} conservative than the elasto-plastic FE reference ($g=0.436$, $\sigma_{\mathrm{VM}}=\SI{86.5}{MPa}$), reversing the apparent non-conservatism identified in Table~\ref{tab:g-comparison}. This confirms that the raw comparison of Table~\ref{tab:g-comparison} is not made on a consistent allowable-stress basis: $g$ and $\sigma_m+\sigma_b$ as computed under the CMS1 rule (RB~3651.1131, allowable $S^{*}$) are not directly comparable to a von Mises stress checked against $1.3\,S_m$, and the code-mandated re-scaling of Eq.~\eqref{eq:pmpb} restores the expected conservative character of the analytical RCC-MRx route. \\

\section{Conclusions}
% =============================================================================

\textcolor{red}{to redo these contents}


\begin{enumerate}
\item The paper provides a consolidated route from the historical background and constitutive assumptions to the practical RCC-MRx definitions of $r_M$ and $g$, supported by the code provisions, figures and derivations presented here.
\item The elastic benchmark shows that the beam-element piping model and the MSC~Marc shell model agree well in terms of global flexibility once the elbow flexibility factor is introduced consistently and the boundary conditions are treated carefully.
\item For the benchmark elbow, the direct RCC-MRx output gives $r_M=2.638$ and $g=0.3237$, whereas the non-linear shell reference yields a higher effective abatement level when the comparison is made on a raw section-stress basis.
\item This apparent discrepancy is resolved by two complementary observations: the code requires the abated secondary effect to be expressed in terms of primary stress, and the shell reference solution is also sensitive to mesh density in the elbow ovalisation zone (see also Appendix~\ref{app:mesh-sensitivity}).
\item The additional wall-thickness variants and the cantilever validation case support the robustness of the implementation and show that the analytical elastic follow-up route can be checked independently of the main elbow benchmark.
\item Modern numerical tools make such comparisons much faster to perform, but they also increase the importance of modelling choices and verification steps, especially those related to mesh density, shell formulation and boundary-condition idealisation.
\item The present work remains limited by the adopted boundary conditions, shell formulation, temperature-invariant Ramberg--Osgood shape parameters, and the omission of creep, stress relaxation and pressure effects. Extending the study to include pressure loading, alternative hardening descriptions and further mesh-convergence work is a natural next step.



\item Several open questions regarding the domain of validity of this simplified, temperature-invariant hardening shape remain and are only partially addressed here: sensitivity to strain rate, monotonic versus cyclic hardening, number of cycles, and environment (air, PWR primary water, sodium, etc.). Figure~\ref{fig:ro-curves} shows the temperature family and parameter sensitivity. At $\SI{0.01}{}$ true strain, the candidate hardening curves differ by about \SI{4.0}{MPa} ($\sim$\SI{5}{\percent}) \cite{NASAn1930}.

\section{\texorpdfstring{}{Appendix \thesection: }Definition of elastic follow-up factor}
\label{ApdxA}

\textbf{Review required.} The following derivations are retained from the source material. Their multiaxial assumptions, equivalent-strain definitions and claimed bounds require further verification; they should not be read as a general proof of the RCC-MRx default method.



The first assumption pertains to loading-path nonlinearity and is based on Kachanov's hypothesis of a radially proportional (self-similar) stress redistribution:
\begin{equation}
\bar{\bar\sigma} = \varphi\,\bar{\bar\sigma}_0 
\label{eq:kachanov-stress}
\end{equation}


Equation ~\eqref{eq:kachanov-stress} might also be expressed for the equivalent stresses:
\begin{align} 
\sigma^* =\varphi \sigma_0^* 
\label{eq:kachanov-stress-equivalent}
\end{align}





$(\Delta\bar{\bar{\varepsilon}}_0, \Delta\bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0)$ denotes the strain--stress tensor state of the material point obtained from a purely elastic analysis. $\varepsilon_0^*$ and $\sigma_0^*$ are the corresponding equivalent elastic strain and stress, respectively (schematically represented by point \textbf{E} in Figure~\ref{fig:deviation}). See Section~\ref{sec:equivalent_sigma_epsilon_eq} for further details.

$(\Delta\bar{\bar{\varepsilon}}, \Delta\bar{\bar{\sigma}})$ denotes the strain--stress tensor state of the material point obtained from an elastoplastic analysis. $\varepsilon^*$ and $\sigma^*$ are the corresponding equivalent elastoplastic strain and stress, respectively (schematically represented by point \textbf{SP} in Figure~\ref{fig:deviation}).






The second assumption concerns material nonlinearity, represented by the Ramberg--Osgood law. RCC-MRx provides the corresponding uniaxial stress--strain relation as follows:
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon_{axial} = \frac{\sigma_{axial}}{E} + \left(\frac{\sigma_{axial}}{K}\right)^{1/n}
\label{eq:Osgood_code}
\end{equation}

\subsection{First approach: Ramberg-Osgood relation under uniaxial configurations}
\label{sec:firstApproach}

In this first approach, we consider only the axial deformation of the beam under an imposed displacement \textbf{u}. All stress and strain tensors are represented by their corresponding axial scalar quantities: \textbf{SP}($\varepsilon$, $\sigma$) and \textbf{E}($\varepsilon_0$, $\sigma_0$).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figures/FirstApproach.png}
\caption{A beam is axially loaded by a secodary load-\textcolor{red}{to adap the figure: cycle displacement deltaU is out scope}}
\label{fig:firstApproach}
\end{figure}

For uniaxial loads, the RCC-MRx Ramberg-Osgood formula can be directly applied:
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon(x) = \frac{\sigma(x)}{E}   + \left(\frac{\sigma(x)}{K}\right)^{1/n}
\label{eq:Osgood-first-approach}
\end{equation}

The Khachanov assumption is simply $\sigma(x)=\varphi \sigma_0(x)$. The Ramberg--Osgood relation is rewritten as follows:
\begin{align} 
\varepsilon(x) = \frac{\varphi \sigma_0(x)}{E} + \left(\frac{\varphi \sigma_0(x)}{K}\right)^{1/n}  = \varphi \varepsilon_0(x)  + \phi \varepsilon_0^{p}(x)
\label{eq:Osgood_modified-C}
\end{align}

where $\varepsilon_0^{p}$ is the equivalent plastic strain obtained from the Ramberg--Osgood law using the elastic stress $\sigma_0$, and $\phi=\varphi^{1/n}$. \\


Now, let's analyze the structure using an elastoplastic approach (point \textbf{SP}):
\begin{equation*}
\int_{x=0}^{x=L} \delta\sigma(x) \varepsilon(x) A(x) \,dx = \delta\sigma(L)A(L)u 
\end{equation*}

Using relation~\eqref{eq:kachanov-stress-equivalent} and introducing the variation of the unknown field, $\delta\varphi$, yields 
\begin{equation*} 
\int_{x=0}^{x=L} (\delta \varphi) \sigma_0(x) \varepsilon(x) A(x) \,dx =(\delta \varphi)\sigma_0(L) A(L) u 
\end{equation*}

\begin{equation} 
\int_{x=0}^{x=L} \sigma_0(x) \varepsilon(x) A(x) \,dx =\sigma_0(L) A(L) u 
\label{eq:EnergyBalance-elastoplastic-A}
\end{equation}


For the elastic solution (point \textbf{E}), consider the same secondary loads \textbf{u}. The principle of virtual work gives 
\begin{equation} 
\frac{1}{2}\int_{x=0}^{x=L} \sigma_0(x)  \varepsilon_0(x) A(x) \, dx = \frac{1}{2}\sigma_0(L) A(L) u 
\label{eq:EnergyBalance-elastic-B}
\end{equation} 

The energy balances for the elastic solution (Equation~\eqref{eq:EnergyBalance-elastic-B}) and the elastoplastic solution (Equation~\eqref{eq:EnergyBalance-elastoplastic-A}) yield \begin{flalign*} 
\int_{x=0}^{x=L} \sigma_0(x) \varepsilon(x) A(x) \,dx &=\int_{x=0}^{x=L} \sigma_0(x)  \varepsilon_0(x) A(x) \, dx
\end{flalign*} 

Substituting Equations~\eqref{eq:kachanov-stress} and ~\eqref{eq:Osgood_modified-C} into the above expression yields 
\begin{flalign*} 
\int_{x=0}^{x=L} \sigma_0(x) \left( \varphi(x) \varepsilon_0(x)  + \phi \varepsilon_0^{p}(x) \right) A(x) \,dx &=\int_{x=0}^{x=L} \sigma_0(x)  \varepsilon_0(x) A(x) \, dx
\end{flalign*} 
\begin{flalign} 
(\varphi-1) \int_{x=0}^{x=L} \sigma_0(x) \varepsilon_0(x) A(x) \, dx + \phi \int_{x=0}^{x=L} \sigma_0(x) \varepsilon_0^p(x) A(x)\, dx &=0
\label{eq:phiiT}
\end{flalign} 


Defining the local resilience t and global resilience T as below:
\begin{equation*} 
\frac{1}{t}= \frac{ \varepsilon_0^p(x)}{ \varepsilon_0(x)}
\end{equation*} 
\begin{equation*} 
\frac{1}{T}= \frac{ \int_{x=0}^{x=L} \sigma_0(x) \varepsilon_0^p(x) A(x) \, dx }{  \int_{x=0}^{x=L} \sigma_0(x) \varepsilon_0(x) A(x) \, dx }
\end{equation*} 
or
\begin{equation*} 
\frac{1}{T}= \frac{ \int_{x=0}^{x=L} \frac{1}{t} \sigma_0^2(x) A(x) \, dx}{\int_{x=0}^{x=L} \sigma_0^2(x) A(x) \, dx}     \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{(if E is homogeneous throughout the structure)}
\end{equation*} 


The above equation ~\eqref{eq:phiiT} can be rewritten as 
\begin{equation}
\left( \varphi-1 \right)T+\phi=0
\label{eq:LaRoche4A} 
\end{equation}

Now, starting from equation ~\eqref{eq:Osgood_modified-C}, we have:
\begin{align} 
\varepsilon(x)  &= \varphi \varepsilon_0(x)  + \phi \varepsilon_0^{p}(x)  \notag \\[3mm]
\varepsilon(x) - \varepsilon_0(x) &=\varepsilon_0(x)  (\varphi-1) \left(1+\frac{\phi}{\varphi-1}\frac{\varepsilon_0^p(x)}{\varepsilon_0(x)}\right) \notag \\[3mm]
\frac{\varepsilon(x)-\varepsilon_0(x)}{ \left(\varphi-1 \right)\varepsilon_0(x)} &= 1+\frac{\phi}{\varphi-1}\frac{\varepsilon_0^p(x)}{\varepsilon_0(x)} \notag \\[3mm]
\frac{E(\varepsilon(x)-\varepsilon_0(x))}{ \left(\varphi-1 \right)\sigma_0(x)} &= 1+\frac{\phi}{\varphi-1}\frac{\varepsilon_0^p(x)}{\varepsilon_0(x)} \notag \\[3mm]
\frac{E(\varepsilon(x)-\varepsilon_0(x))}{ \sigma(x)-\sigma_0(x)} &= 1+\frac{\phi}{\varphi-1}\frac{\varepsilon_0^p(x)}{\varepsilon_0(x)} \notag \\[3mm]
\frac{E(\varepsilon(x)-\varepsilon_0(x))}{ \sigma_0(x)-\sigma(x)} &= -1+\frac{T}{t} \text{             \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \    (from equation ~\eqref{eq:LaRoche4A})  }
\end{align}




Now we define the elastic-follow-up factor $rM=\frac{E(\varepsilon(x) - \varepsilon_0(x)}{ \sigma_0(x)-\sigma(x) }  $, then:
	\begin{equation}
		rM    =\frac{T}{t} -1
		\label{def:EFU}
	\end{equation}

\subsection{Definition of equivalent stress and strain}
\label{sec:equivalent_sigma_epsilon_eq}

The equivalent stress and equivalent strain for the purely elastic solution are defined as
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon_0^* = \sqrt{\frac{2}{3}e_{0ij}e_{0ij}}, \qquad e_{0ij} = \varepsilon_{0ij} - \frac{1}{3}\varepsilon_{0kk}\delta_{ij}, \label{eij}
\end{equation}
and
\begin{equation}
\sigma_0^* = \sqrt{\frac{3}{2}s_{0ij}s_{0ij}}, \qquad s_{0ij} = \sigma_{0ij} - \frac{1}{3}\sigma_{0kk}\delta_{ij}. \label{sij}
\end{equation}
Using Hooke's law $\varepsilon_{0ij} = \frac{1+\nu}{E}\sigma_{0ij} - \frac{\nu}{E}\sigma_{0kk}\delta_{ij} $, and consider the trace of Hook's tensorial relation, we have:
\begin{equation*}
\varepsilon_{0kk} = \frac{1+\nu}{E}\sigma_{0kk} - 3\frac{\nu}{E}\sigma_{0kk} = \frac{1-2\nu}{E}\sigma_{0kk}
\end{equation*}
Substituting the trace and tensorial relation of the Hook law into Equation~\eqref{eij} yields
\begin{equation*}
e_{0ij} = \frac{1+\nu}{E}\sigma_{0ij} - \frac{\nu}{E}\sigma_{0kk}\delta_{ij} - \frac{1}{3}\frac{1-2\nu}{E}\sigma_{0kk}\delta_{ij}. \label{eij3}
\end{equation*}
After simplification,
\begin{equation*}
e_{0ij} = \frac{1+\nu}{E} \left( \sigma_{0ij} - \frac{1}{3}\sigma_{0kk}\delta_{ij} \right) = \frac{1+\nu}{E}s_{0ij}.
\end{equation*}
Using the above relation, equations~\eqref{eij} and \eqref{sij}, the equivalent strain is related to the equivalent stress by
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon_0^* = \frac{2}{3}\frac{1+\nu}{E}\sigma_0^*.
\label{e_final}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Second approach: Generalized stress-strain tensors Ramberg-Osgood under multiaxial configurations}
\label{sec:secondApproach}

In context of a multiaxial configuration, the Ramberg Osgood law is generalized for the plastic deformation tensor:
\begin{align} 
\bar{\bar\varepsilon}^p(\bar{\bar\sigma}) &= \phi(\varphi)  \bar{\bar\varepsilon}^p(\bar{\bar\sigma}_0)  \notag \\[3mm]
\bar{\bar\varepsilon}^p &= \phi(\varphi)  \bar{\bar\varepsilon}_0^p
\label{eq:plastic_tensor}
\end{align}

The total elastoplastic strain tensor is expressed as below:
\begin{equation*} 
\bar{\bar\varepsilon} = \bar{\bar\varepsilon}^{el} +\bar{\bar\varepsilon}^p
\end{equation*}
Following Hooke' law $\bar{\bar\sigma}=\bar{\bar C} \bar{\bar\varepsilon}^{el}$ and equation ~\eqref{eq:plastic_tensor}, we have:
\begin{equation} 
\bar{\bar\varepsilon} =\bar{\bar C}^{-1} \bar{\bar\sigma} +\phi  \bar{\bar\varepsilon}_0^p=\varphi \bar{\bar C}^{-1} \bar{\bar\sigma}_0 +\phi  \bar{\bar\varepsilon}_0^p =\varphi  \bar{\bar\varepsilon}_0 +\phi  \bar{\bar\varepsilon}_0^p
\label{eq:Ramberg-Osgood-modif-2nd}
\end{equation}

Equation ~\eqref{eq:Ramberg-Osgood-modif-2nd} might also be expressed for the equivalent strains:
\begin{align} 
\varepsilon^* =\varphi \varepsilon_0^* + \phi \varepsilon_0^{p*} 
\label{eq:Ramberg-Osgood-modif-2nd-equivalent}
\end{align}


\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figures/energy_balance_equivalent_stress_scheme.png}
\caption{Energy-balance scheme illustrating how the virtual-work statement of the elastic calculation leads to the equivalent stress--strain formulation used to define $T$, $t$ and ultimately $r_M$.}
\label{fig:energy-balance-scheme}
\end{figure}




For the elastoplastic analysis (point \textbf{SP}), the local equilibrium equation is given by $\sigma_{ij,j}+f_i=0$ (with $i,j=\overline{1,3}$). \\
Considering an imposed displacement field $\vec u$ prescribed on the boundary of the domain, together with a thermal expansion load $\bar{\bar\epsilon}$ acting on all material points (see Figure \ref{fig:energy-balance-scheme}), application of the divergence theorem and the principle of virtual work leads to the following relation involving an infinitesimal variation of the stress field, $\delta\sigma$:
\begin{equation}
\iiint \delta\bar{\bar\sigma}:\bar{\bar\varepsilon}\,dV = \iint \delta\bar{\bar\sigma}\,\vec n\,\vec u\,dS - \iiint \delta\bar{\bar\sigma}:\bar{\bar\epsilon}\,dV
\label{eq:roche6}
\end{equation}
Using relation~\eqref{eq:kachanov-stress-equivalent} and introducing the variation of the unknown field, $\delta\varphi$, yields 
\begin{equation*} \iiint_V (\delta\varphi)\,\bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\varepsilon}}\,dV = \iint_S (\delta\varphi)\,\bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 \cdot \vec{n} \cdot \vec{u}\,dS - \iiint_V (\delta\varphi)\,\bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\epsilon}}\,dV
\end{equation*}


\begin{equation} \iiint_V \bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\varepsilon}}\,dV = \iint_S \bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 \cdot \vec{n} \cdot \vec{u}\,dS - \iiint_V\bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\epsilon}}\,dV
\label{eq:EnergyBalance-elastoplastic-2nd}
\end{equation}


For the elastic solution (point \textbf{E}), consider the same secondary loads ($\vec{u}$ and $\bar{\bar{\epsilon}}$). The principle of virtual work gives 
\begin{equation} 
\frac{1}{2}\iiint_V \bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\varepsilon}}_0 \, dV = \frac{1}{2}\iint_S \bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 \cdot \vec{n} \cdot \vec{u} \, dS - \frac{1}{2}\iiint_V \bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\epsilon}} \, dV \label{eq:EnergyBalance-elastic-2nd}
\end{equation} 

The energy balances for the elastic solution (Equation~\eqref{eq:EnergyBalance-elastic-2nd}) and the elastoplastic solution (Equation~\eqref{eq:EnergyBalance-elastoplastic-2nd}) yield \begin{flalign*} 
\iiint_V \bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\varepsilon}} \, dV &= \iiint_V \bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\varepsilon}}_0 \, dV, \\ \iiint_V \frac{1}{\varphi}\, \bar{\bar{\sigma}} : \bar{\bar{\varepsilon}} \, dV &= \iiint_V \bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\varepsilon}}_0 \, dV
\end{flalign*} 

Neglecting the contribution of volumetric strains to the strain energy, the above tensorial relation can be expressed in terms of the equivalent stress and equivalent strain as
\begin{flalign*}
\iiint_V \frac{1}{\varphi}\, \sigma^* \varepsilon^* \, dV = \iiint_V \sigma_0^* \varepsilon_0^* \, dV
\end{flalign*} 

Substituting Equations~\eqref{eq:Ramberg-Osgood-modif-2nd-equivalent} and ~\eqref{eq:kachanov-stress-equivalent} into the above expression yields 
\begin{flalign*} 
\iiint_V \sigma_0^* \left(  \varphi \varepsilon_0^* + \phi \varepsilon_0^{p*}  \right) \, dV &= \iiint_V \sigma_0^* \varepsilon_0^* \, dV
\end{flalign*} 
\begin{flalign} 
\left( \varphi-1 \right) \iiint_V \sigma_0^* \varepsilon_0^* \, dV + \phi \iiint_V \sigma_0^* \varepsilon_0^{p*} \, dV &=0
\label{eq:phiiT-2nd}
\end{flalign} 



Defining the local resilience t and global resilience T as below:
\begin{equation*} 
\frac{1}{t}= \frac{ \varepsilon_0^{p*}}{ \varepsilon_0^*}
\end{equation*} 
\begin{equation*} 
\frac{1}{T}= \frac{ \iiint_V \sigma_0^* \varepsilon_0^{p*} \, dV }{ \iiint_V \sigma_0^* \varepsilon_0^* \, dV }
\end{equation*} 
or
\begin{equation*} 
\frac{1}{T}=\frac{ \iiint_V \frac{1}{t} \sigma_0^{*2} \, dV }{ \iiint_V \sigma_0^{*2}  \, dV }    \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{(if E is homogeneous throughout the structure)}
\end{equation*} 


The above equation \eqref{eq:phiiT-2nd} can be rewritten as 
\begin{equation}\left( \varphi-1 \right)T+\phi=0
\label{eq:LaRoche4} 
\end{equation}


Now, starting from equation ~\eqref{eq:Ramberg-Osgood-modif-2nd-equivalent}, we have:
\begin{align} 
\varepsilon^* &= \varphi \varepsilon_0^* + \phi \varepsilon_0^{p*}  \notag \\[3mm]
\varepsilon^* - \varepsilon_0^* &=\varepsilon_0^*  \left(\varphi-1 \right) \left(1+\frac{\phi}{\varphi-1}\frac{\varepsilon_0^{p*}}{\varepsilon_0^*}\right) \notag \\[3mm]
\frac{\varepsilon^*-\varepsilon_0^*}{ \left(\varphi-1 \right)\varepsilon_0^*} &= 1+\frac{\phi}{\varphi-1}\frac{\varepsilon_0^{p*}}{\varepsilon_0^*} \notag \\[3mm]
\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \frac{\varepsilon^*-\varepsilon_0^*} { (\varphi-1) \left( \frac{2}{3}\frac{1+\nu}{E}  \sigma_0^* \right) }  &= 1+\frac{\phi}{\varphi-1}\frac{\varepsilon_0^{p*}}{\varepsilon_0^*} \notag  \qquad \text{(from equation \eqref{e_final}, see Section \ref{sec:equivalent_sigma_epsilon_eq})} \notag \\[3mm]
\frac{E(\varepsilon^*-\varepsilon_0^*)} { (\varphi-1) \sigma_0^*} \frac{3}{2(1+\nu)} &= 1 +\frac{\phi}{\varphi-1}\frac{\varepsilon_0^{p*}}{\varepsilon_0^*}  \notag  \\[3mm]
\frac{E(\varepsilon^*-\varepsilon_0^*)} {\sigma_0^*-\sigma^*} &=0.867 \left( -1-\frac{\phi}{\varphi-1}\frac{\varepsilon_0^{p*}}{\varepsilon_0^*} \right) \notag \\[3mm]
\frac{E(\varepsilon^*-\varepsilon_0^*)} {\sigma_0^*-\sigma^*} &=  0.867 \left( \frac{T}{t} -1      \right)
\end{align}

Now we define the elastic-follow-up factor $rM=\frac{E(\varepsilon^* - \varepsilon_0^*)}{ \sigma_0^*-\sigma^* }  $, then:
	\begin{equation}
		rM    =0.867\left( \frac{T}{t} -1      \right) < \frac{T}{t} -1
		\label{def:EFU-2nd}
	\end{equation}

\subsection{Third approach: Equivalent stress--strain relation Ramberg-Osgood relation under multiaxial configurations}
\label{sec:thirdApproach}





For multidirection loads, the Ramberg-Osgood relation is generalized as below:
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon^* = \frac{\sigma^*}{E}   + \left(\frac{\sigma^*}{K}\right)^{1/n}
\label{eq:Osgood}
\end{equation}



By converting the tensorial relation of Equation~\ref{eq:kachanov-stress} into its equivalent stress--strain form, the Ramberg--Osgood relation can be rewritten as follows:
\begin{align} 
\varepsilon^* &= \frac{\varphi \sigma_0^*}{E} + \left(\frac{\varphi \sigma_0^*}{K}\right)^{1/n} \notag \\
&= \varphi \frac{3\varepsilon_0^*}{2(1+\nu)} + \varphi^{1/n} \left(\frac{\sigma_0^*}{K}\right)^{1/n} \notag \\
&= \varphi \frac{3\varepsilon_0^*}{2(1+\nu)}  + \phi \varepsilon_0^{p*} 
\label{eq:Osgood_modified}
\end{align}

With $\varepsilon_0^{p*} $ is the equivalent plastic deformation obtained by Ramberg-Osgood law using the elastic stress $\sigma_0^*$. \\








For the elastoplastic analysis (point \textbf{SP}), the local equilibrium equation is given by $\sigma_{ij,j}+f_i=0$ (with $i,j=\overline{1,3}$). \\
Considering an imposed displacement field $\vec u$ prescribed on the boundary of the domain, together with a thermal expansion load $\bar{\bar\epsilon}$ acting on all material points (see Figure \ref{fig:energy-balance-scheme}), application of the divergence theorem and the principle of virtual work leads to the following relation involving an infinitesimal variation of the stress field, $\delta\sigma$:
\begin{equation}
\iiint \delta\bar{\bar\sigma}:\bar{\bar\varepsilon}\,dV = \iint \delta\bar{\bar\sigma}\,\vec n\,\vec u\,dS - \iiint \delta\bar{\bar\sigma}:\bar{\bar\epsilon}\,dV
\label{eq:roche6}
\end{equation}
Using relation~\eqref{eq:kachanov-stress} and introducing the variation of the unknown field, $\delta\varphi$, yields 
\begin{equation*} \iiint_V (\delta\varphi)\,\bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\varepsilon}}\,dV = \iint_S (\delta\varphi)\,\bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 \cdot \vec{n} \cdot \vec{u}\,dS - \iiint_V (\delta\varphi)\,\bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\epsilon}}\,dV
\end{equation*}


\begin{equation} \iiint_V \bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\varepsilon}}\,dV = \iint_S \bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 \cdot \vec{n} \cdot \vec{u}\,dS - \iiint_V\bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\epsilon}}\,dV
\label{eq:EnergyBalance-elastoplastic}
\end{equation}


For the elastic solution (point \textbf{E}), consider the same secondary loads ($\vec{u}$ and $\bar{\bar{\epsilon}}$). The principle of virtual work gives 
\begin{equation} 
\frac{1}{2}\iiint_V \bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\varepsilon}}_0 \, dV = \frac{1}{2}\iint_S \bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 \cdot \vec{n} \cdot \vec{u} \, dS - \frac{1}{2}\iiint_V \bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\epsilon}} \, dV \label{eq:EnergyBalance-elastic}
\end{equation} 

The energy balances for the elastic solution (Equation~\eqref{eq:EnergyBalance-elastic}) and the elastoplastic solution (Equation~\eqref{eq:EnergyBalance-elastoplastic}) yield \begin{flalign*} 
\iiint_V \bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\varepsilon}} \, dV &= \iiint_V \bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\varepsilon}}_0 \, dV, \\ \iiint_V \frac{1}{\varphi}\, \bar{\bar{\sigma}} : \bar{\bar{\varepsilon}} \, dV &= \iiint_V \bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\varepsilon}}_0 \, dV
\end{flalign*} 

Neglecting the contribution of volumetric strains to the strain energy, the above tensorial relation can be expressed in terms of the equivalent stress and equivalent strain as
\begin{flalign*}
\iiint_V \frac{1}{\varphi}\, \sigma^* \varepsilon^* \, dV = \iiint_V \sigma_0^* \varepsilon_0^* \, dV
\end{flalign*} 

Substituting Equations~\eqref{eq:kachanov-stress} and ~\eqref{eq:Osgood_modified} into the above expression yields 
\begin{flalign*} 
\iiint_V \sigma_0^* \left(  \varphi \frac{3\varepsilon_0^*}{2(1+\nu)}  + \phi \varepsilon_0^{p*}  \right) \, dV &= \iiint_V \sigma_0^* \varepsilon_0^* \, dV
\end{flalign*} 
\begin{flalign*} 
\left( \varphi\frac{3}{2(1+\nu)}-1 \right) \iiint_V \sigma_0^* \varepsilon_0^* \, dV + \phi \iiint_V \sigma_0^* \varepsilon_0^{p*} \, dV &=0
\end{flalign*} 




Defining the global resilience T as below:
\begin{equation*} 
\frac{1}{T}= \frac{ \iiint_V \sigma_0^* \varepsilon_0^{p*} \, dV }{ \iiint_V \sigma_0^* \varepsilon_0^* \, dV }
\end{equation*} 
The above equation can be rewritten as 
\begin{equation}\left( \varphi\frac{3}{2(1+\nu)}-1 \right)T+\phi=0
\label{eq:LaRoche4} 
\end{equation}


Now, starting from equation ~\eqref{eq:Osgood_modified}, we have:
\begin{align} 
\varepsilon^* &= \varphi \frac{3\varepsilon_0^*}{2(1+\nu)}  + \phi \varepsilon_0^{p*}  \notag \\[3mm]
\varepsilon^* - \varepsilon_0^* &=\varepsilon_0^*  \left(\varphi\frac{3}{2(1+\nu)}-1 \right) \left(1+\frac{\phi}{\varphi\frac{3}{2(1+\nu)}-1}\frac{\varepsilon_0^{p*}}{\varepsilon_0^*}\right) \notag \\[3mm]
\frac{\varepsilon^*-\varepsilon_0^*}{ \left(\varphi\frac{3}{2(1+\nu)}-1 \right)\varepsilon_0^*} &= 1+\frac{\phi}{\varphi\frac{3}{2(1+\nu)}-1}\frac{\varepsilon_0^{p*}}{\varepsilon_0^*} \notag \\[3mm]
\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \frac{\varepsilon^*-\varepsilon_0^*} { \left(\varphi\frac{3}{2(1+\nu)}-1 \right) \left( \frac{2}{3}\frac{1+\nu}{E}  \sigma_0^* \right) }  &= 1+\frac{\phi}{\varphi\frac{3}{2(1+\nu)}-1}\frac{\varepsilon_0^{p*}}{\varepsilon_0^*} \qquad \text{(from equation \eqref{e_final}, see Section \ref{sec:equivalent_sigma_epsilon_eq})} \notag \\[3mm]
\frac{E(\varepsilon^*-\varepsilon_0^*)} {\varphi \sigma_0^*- \frac{2(1+\nu)}{3}\sigma_0^*} &= 1+\frac{\phi}{\varphi\frac{3}{2(1+\nu)}-1}\frac{\varepsilon_0^{p*}}{\varepsilon_0^*} \notag  \\[3mm]
\frac{E(\varepsilon^*-\varepsilon_0^*)} {0.867\sigma_0^*-\sigma^*} &= -1-\frac{\phi}{\varphi\frac{3}{2(1+\nu)}-1}\frac{\varepsilon_0^{p*}}{\varepsilon_0^*} \notag
\end{align}



With the local resilience defined as $t=\frac{\varepsilon_0^*}{\varepsilon_0^{p*}}$ and using equation ~\eqref{eq:LaRoche4}, the above formula becomes:
	\begin{equation*}
		\frac{E(\varepsilon^*-\varepsilon_0^*)} {0.867\sigma_0^*-\sigma^*} =    \frac{T}{t} -1      
	\end{equation*}
Now we define the elastic-follow-up factor $rM=\frac{E(\varepsilon^* - \varepsilon_0^*)}{ \sigma_0^*-\sigma^* }  $, then:
\begin{equation*}
	rM    < \frac{E(\varepsilon^*-\varepsilon_0^*)} {0.867\sigma_0^*-\sigma^*} 
\end{equation*}
or 
\begin{equation}
	rM    <  \frac{T}{t}-1 
	\label{def:EFU-3rd}
\end{equation}

This section continues the discussion presented in §\ref{sec:EFF}. With the objective of generalizing the elastic follow-up concept and quantifying the degree of elastic follow-up in a piping system subjected to loading, Roland L. Roche introduced two fundamental assumptions. All details of calculations are provided in Appendix \ref{ApdxA}. \\
The first assumption is the Kachanov hypothesis, which assumes that the elastoplastic stress field (point \textbf{SP}) is proportional to the elastic stress field (point \textbf{E}) through a scalar  $\varphi$, taken to be uniform throughout the entire structure under consideration. See equations \eqref{eq:kachanov-stress} and \eqref{eq:kachanov-stress-equivalent}.\\
The second hypothesis concerns the constitutive law and accounts for material plasticity through the Ramberg--Osgood relation through equations \eqref{eq:Osgood-first-approach}, \eqref{eq:plastic_tensor} and \eqref{eq:Osgood}. \\

\vspace{1cm}
\textbf{First approach:} \\
As a first step, Roland L. Roche considers the axial behavior of a straight pipe under displacement-imposed load. The detailed derivation is provided in Appendix \ref{sec:firstApproach}. Using the principle of virtual work, a relationship is established between the purely elastic solution and the elastoplastic solution through the elastic follow-up factor $r_M$, defined as
\[
r_M=\frac{E\left(\varepsilon(x)-\varepsilon_0(x)\right)}
{\sigma_0(x)-\sigma(x)}.
\]
This definition of $r_M$ corresponds to the inverse of the slope of the segment joining points \textbf{E} and \textbf{SP} in Figure \ref{fig:deviation}.
The energy balance helps to map between point \textbf{E} and point \textbf{SP} by the following formula of rM:
\begin{equation*}
	rM    =\frac{T}{t} -1
\end{equation*}
With local resilience (t) and global resilience (T) defined as below:
\begin{equation*} 
\frac{1}{t}= \frac{ \varepsilon_0^p(x)}{ \varepsilon_0(x)}
\end{equation*} 
\begin{equation*} 
\frac{1}{T}= \frac{ \int_{x=0}^{x=L} \sigma_0(x) \varepsilon_0^p(x) A(x) \, dx }{  \int_{x=0}^{x=L} \sigma_0(x) \varepsilon_0(x) A(x) \, dx }
\end{equation*} 
or 
\begin{equation*} 
\frac{1}{T}=\frac{ \int_{x=0}^{x=L} \frac{1}{t} \sigma_0^2(x) A(x) \, dx}{\int_{x=0}^{x=L} \sigma_0^2(x) A(x) \, dx}     \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{(if E is homogeneous throughout the structure)}
\end{equation*} 

Local resilience and global resilience are indicators of the inverse degree of plasticity present in a structure, at the local and global scales, respectively. Of particular interest is the fact that both \(t\) and \(T\) are determined solely from the results of a linear elastic analysis (point \textbf{E}). In other words, determining \(r_M\) requires only a linear elastic analysis together with knowledge of the material's plastic constitutive law.

Knowing \(r_M\), which corresponds to the inverse of the slope of the EFU segment (see Figure \ref{fig:deviation}), and using the Ramberg--Osgood relation implicitly defines a system of two equations. Solving this system makes it possible to determine the position of point \textbf{SP} from the elastic solution represented by point \textbf{E}. The detailed calculations are provided in Appendix \ref{application_roche}.

Moreover, the elastic follow-up factor \(r_M\) provides a means of quantifying the degree of elastic follow-up in a generalized piping system without explicitly considering spring stiffness. The higher the value of \(r_M\), the greater the degree of elastic follow-up present in the structure. The parametric study of the spring effect presented in Figure \ref{fig:parametric-EFU} is revisited in Appendix \ref{application_roche}, this time using \(r_M\) as the observing parameter. The results demonstrate that, for the simple case of a straight pipe with a variable cross-section, the objective outlined at the end of §\ref{sec:EFF} is indeed achieved.

\textbf{Second approach:} \\
As a second step, Roland L. Roche extends the derivation to a general piping system. In addition to displacement-controlled loading, thermal expansion effects are also taken into account. The details of the derivation are provided in Appendix \ref{sec:secondApproach}. The uniaxial Ramberg--Osgood law is generalized to the tensorial framework through Equation \eqref{eq:plastic_tensor}:
\begin{align*}
\bar{\bar\varepsilon}^p &= \phi(\varphi)\,\bar{\bar\varepsilon}_0^p
\end{align*}
To remain consistent with the stress--strain representation of Figure \ref{fig:deviation}, the tensorial formulation must then be recast in terms of equivalent stress and equivalent strain quantities. Following this conversion, the same procedure as in the first approach is applied. The elastic follow-up factor and the associated quantities are obtained as follows:
\begin{equation*}
r_M=\frac{E\left(\varepsilon^*-\varepsilon_0^*\right)}
{\sigma_0^*-\sigma^*}
\end{equation*}

\begin{equation*}
r_M = 0.867\left(\frac{T}{t}-1\right)
< \frac{T}{t}-1
\end{equation*}

\begin{equation*}
\frac{1}{t}=
\frac{\varepsilon_0^{p*}}
{\varepsilon_0^*}
\end{equation*}

\begin{equation*}
\frac{1}{T}
=
\frac{\iiint_V \sigma_0^* \varepsilon_0^{p*}\,dV}
{\iiint_V \sigma_0^* \varepsilon_0^*\,dV}
\end{equation*}

or

\begin{equation*}
\frac{1}{T} = \frac{\iiint_V \frac{1}{t}\sigma_0^{*2}\,dV}{\iiint_V \sigma_0^{*2}\,dV}    \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{(if E is homogeneous throughout the structure)}
\end{equation*}




Once again, a relationship very similar to that obtained in the first approach is found for \(r_M\), differing only by the factor 0.867. However, the formulation is now generalized to arbitrary piping systems and accounts for multiaxial stress states, displacement-controlled loading, and thermal expansion effects.

\vspace{1cm}
\textbf{Third approach:} \\
Finally, in a third step, the method developed by Roland L. Roche is generalized by removing the need for the tensorial Ramberg--Osgood assumption and relying solely on equivalent stress--strain relations:
\begin{equation*}
\varepsilon^* = \frac{\sigma^*}{E} + \left(\frac{\sigma^*}{K}\right)^{1/n}
\end{equation*}
Following the same methodology as Roland L. Roche, the following relationships are obtained:
\begin{equation*}
r_M=\frac{E\left(\varepsilon^*-\varepsilon_0^*\right)}
{\sigma_0^*-\sigma^*}
\end{equation*}
\begin{equation*}
r_M <
\frac{E\left(\varepsilon^*-\varepsilon_0^*\right)}
{0.867\,\sigma_0^*-\sigma^*}
\end{equation*}
or equivalently,
\begin{equation}
r_M < \frac{T}{t}-1
\label{def:EFU-3rd-overview}
\end{equation}
where \(T\) and \(t\) are defined exactly as in the second approach.

Compared with the method of Roland L. Roche, the resulting expression is no longer an equality but an upper bound on the elastic follow-up factor. Nevertheless, it retains the same interpretation and relies on the same resilience indicators \(t\) and \(T\).

The factor $r_M$ has the following limiting behaviour:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $r_M = 0$ ($T=t$): no elastic follow-up; the stress is purely secondary (fully self-limiting).
    \item $r_M \to \infty$ ($T \gg t$): very significant elastic follow-up; the stress behaves as purely primary.
\end{itemize}
Note that a \emph{higher} $r_M$ is treated by the code as a \emph{more conservative}, more primary-like, assumption; taking $r_M(\mathrm{coeff}=1) > r_M(\mathrm{coeff}=0.867)$ is therefore itself a conservative simplification.

In the Roche three-bar analogy, Bar 2 (no spring) corresponds to $r_M=0$, Bar 1 (rigid connection) corresponds to $r_M\to\infty$, and Bar 3 (elastic spring) lies at an intermediate, finite value of $r_M$ that depends on the spring stiffness $k_2$.

\subsection{Analytical validation on a cantilever with imposed end displacement}

Carrying out the integrals of Eq.~\eqref{eq:Tglobal} in closed form for this linear stress distribution gives the elastic follow-up factor as an explicit function of position,
\begin{equation}
r(x) \;=\; \frac{T}{t}-1 \;=\; \frac{1+2n_0}{3n_0}\left(\frac{x}{l}\right)^{\frac{1-n_0}{n_0}} - 1 ,
\label{eq:cantilever-rx}
\end{equation}
which is monotonically increasing towards the clamp and is therefore maximised at the clamped end ($x=l$), giving the closed-form bound
\begin{equation}
r_{\max} \;=\; \frac{1-n_0}{3n_0} .
\label{eq:cantilever-rmax}
\end{equation}
For the benchmark material ($n_0=0.1125$), the same closed-form development gives the global resilience quantity $T=0.444$, and Eqs.~\eqref{eq:cantilever-rx}--\eqref{eq:cantilever-rmax} then give $r_{\max}=2.63$. Using the no-pressure RCC-MRx expression for the abatement coefficient on that analytical solution yields $g=0.3118$, i.e.\ essentially the same value as the production-code result $g=0.3116$ reported below. Figures~\ref{fig:cantilever-3d}, \ref{fig:cantilever-vm} and \ref{fig:hexagon-cantilever-table} show, respectively, the FE model, the non-linear stress field and the production-code output used to validate this closed-form result.
