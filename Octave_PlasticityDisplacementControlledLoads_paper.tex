\begin{abstract}
Displacement-controlled (secondary) loads in nuclear piping systems are self-limiting and are therefore assessed less severely than primary loads. In RCC-MRx, this distinction is formalised through the \emph{elastic follow-up factor} $r_M$ and the associated \emph{abatement coefficient} $g$, which reduce the displacement-dependent moment when material plasticity is taken into account. This paper consolidates the historical and theoretical background of the method --- from beam theory, elbow ovalisation and Markl's fatigue framework to Roche's elastic follow-up construction --- and benchmarks the RCC-MRx analytical procedure (RB~3643 and RB~3651) against a full elasto-plastic shell finite-element analysis of a simple elbow-and-straight-pipe test case. The elastic benchmark is first calibrated between a beam-based piping code (Aspect Nuclear Pipe Stress) and an MSC~Marc shell model through the elbow flexibility factor $k$. The non-linear benchmark then uses a Ramberg--Osgood constitutive law representative of 316L(N) stainless steel at \SI{550}{\celsius}. The RCC-MRx result ($r_M=2.638$, $g=0.3237$) is compared with the FE-based interpretation of the critical-section response ($g\approx0.436$, $\sigma_{\mathrm{VM}}=\SI{86.5}{MPa}$). The apparent discrepancy is shown to arise from both the code-mandated conversion to primary-equivalent stress and the mesh sensitivity of the shell reference solution. Additional validation is provided by wall-thickness variants and by a cantilever case with a closed-form solution for $r_M$. The paper concludes by identifying the main modelling limitations and the implications for future pressurised benchmarks.
\end{abstract}

\noindent\textbf{Keywords:} RCC-MRx; elastic follow-up; abatement coefficient; displacement-controlled loading; piping stress analysis; Ramberg--Osgood law.

\section{Introduction}
% =============================================================================

Piping stress analysis distinguishes between \emph{primary} loads (pressure, weight, seismic inertia, etc.), which are not self-limiting and can lead to unbounded deformation if the material yields, and \emph{secondary} (displacement-controlled) loads, such as thermal expansion, which are self-limiting: once the material yields locally, the associated stresses relax and the deformation compatibility is preserved elastically or elasto-plastically by the remainder of the structure. Design codes therefore allow secondary stresses to be evaluated with more permissive criteria than primary stresses, provided the self-limiting character of the load is correctly represented.

The RCC-MRx code for high-temperature nuclear components formalises this distinction through the concept of the \emph{abatement coefficient} $g$ (RB~3651) \cite{RCCMRx}, which reduces the displacement-dependent moment used in the primary-type damage assessment. The coefficient $g$ is itself derived from the \emph{elastic follow-up factor} $r_M$ (RB~3643), a structural indicator of how much a locally yielding cross-section is elastically restrained (``followed up'') by the surrounding, comparatively stiffer, structure.

This work has two objectives:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item to present, in a self-contained manner, the historical and theoretical background that leads from classical beam theory to the modern treatment of elastic follow-up and the abatement coefficient in RCC-MRx;
    \item to benchmark the RCC-MRx analytical procedure against a reference elasto-plastic finite-element (FE) shell analysis on a simple, well-defined elbow test case, in order to quantify how conservative (or non-conservative) the code procedure is with respect to a full non-linear reference solution.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Beam theory and flexibility of curved pipes}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Euler--Bernoulli and Timoshenko beam models}

The classical Euler--Bernoulli hypothesis neglects transverse shear deformation and rotational inertia; it is only valid for slender members. The Timoshenko beam theory retains both transverse shear and all inertia terms and remains valid for stockier members as well. For in-plane bending of a beam in the $(x,z)$ plane with constant cross-section and material along the longitudinal axis, the governing equations differ by the inclusion of (i) the rotational inertia associated with bending, (ii) the transverse shear flexibility (Timoshenko hypothesis), and (iii) the coupling between rotational and translational inertia \cite{Flejou2024}. Standard nuclear piping codes rely on Timoshenko-type beam elements for the straight and curved segments of the model. The corresponding governing partial differential equation for the transverse displacement $w(x,t)$ of a Timoshenko beam, including rotational inertia and shear-rotation inertia coupling, is given by
\begin{equation}
E I_y \, \frac{\partial^4 w}{\partial x^4} + \rho S \, \frac{\partial^2 w}{\partial t^2} - \rho I_y \left[1 + \frac{E}{k_z G}\right] \frac{\partial^4 w}{\partial x^2 \, \partial t^2} + \frac{\rho^2 I_y}{k_z G} \, \frac{\partial^4 w}{\partial t^4} - t_{z_{ext}} = 0,
\label{eq:timoshenko}
\end{equation}
where $E$ is Young's modulus, $I_y$ the second moment of area, $\rho$ the mass density, $S$ the cross-sectional area, $k_z$ the shear correction factor, $G$ the shear modulus, and $t_{z_{ext}}$ the externally applied transverse load per unit length. When rotational inertia and shear-rotation inertia coupling are neglected (the classical Euler--Bernoulli specialisation, retaining only the distributed transverse load and the translational inertia term), Eq.~\eqref{eq:timoshenko} reduces to the familiar quasi-static beam equation used for the piping flexibility calculations of the remainder of this section,
\begin{equation}
\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}\left(E I_y \frac{\partial^2 w}{\partial x^2}\right) + \rho S \frac{\partial^2 w}{\partial t^2} - t_{z_{ext}} = 0.
\label{eq:eulerbernoulli}
\end{equation}

\subsection{Elbow ovalisation and flexibility factors}

Under in-plane bending, a curved pipe (elbow) undergoes cross-sectional \emph{ovalisation}: the circular cross-section distorts into an oval shape. This ovalisation, illustrated by the shell result in Figure~\ref{fig:ovalisation}, increases the flexibility of the elbow well beyond the prediction of classical (straight) beam theory, Eq.~\eqref{eq:eulerbernoulli}. The \emph{flexibility factor} $k$ is defined as the ratio between the rotation per unit length of the curved component and the rotation per unit length of an equivalent straight pipe under the same bending moment, as illustrated in Figure~\ref{fig:kfactor-definition}: a straight length $l$ under moment $M$ rotates by $\theta$, whereas the same length of curved component under the same moment rotates by $k\theta$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.75\textwidth]{figures/kfactor_definition_diagrams.png}
\caption{Graphical definition of the flexibility factor $k$, reproduced from the source presentation: a straight length $l$ under end moment $M$ rotates by $\theta$ (left), while the same length of curved component under the same moment rotates by $k\theta$ (right).}
\label{fig:kfactor-definition}
\end{figure}

The source presentation first recalls the older von~K\'arm\'an/Hovgaard elbow expression
\begin{equation}
k = \frac{12h^{2}+10}{12h^{2}+1},
\end{equation}
then introduces Beskin's simpler reference approximation. In the slide sequence, the latter is presented as the practical formula retained for piping calculations, without an explicit intermediate derivation from the former. The flexibility factor of a smooth, unpressurised elbow is therefore written in the benchmark, in terms of the non-dimensional flexibility characteristic $h$, as
\begin{equation}
h = \frac{t\,R}{r^{2}}, \qquad k = \frac{1.65}{h} = \frac{1.65\, r^{2}}{t\,R},
\label{eq:flexfactor}
\end{equation}
where $t$ is the wall thickness, $r$ the mean cross-sectional radius, and $R$ the bend radius.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figures/elbow_ovalisation_contour.png}
\caption{Finite-element illustration of elbow cross-section ovalisation under in-plane bending: the stress concentration follows the ovalised region at the crown of the bend.}
\label{fig:ovalisation}
\end{figure}

For the elbow considered in the benchmark of this paper (outer diameter $D=\SI{219.07}{mm}$, thickness $t=\SI{3.76}{mm}$, bend radius $R=\SI{500}{mm}$, hence $r = \tfrac12(D-t) = \SI{107.66}{mm}$), Eq.~\eqref{eq:flexfactor} gives $h=0.1622$ and $k=10.17$, in agreement with the value used in the benchmark (Section~\ref{sec:benchmark}).

\paragraph{Link with Stress Intensification Factors (SIFs).} Markl observed that the flexibility factor $k$ and the stress intensification factor $i$ (Section~\ref{sec:sif}) are governed by the same underlying physics --- the ovalisation of the cross-section --- and that the inaccuracies affecting $k$ and $i$ tend to compensate one another. This observation justifies the continued use of simple, closed-form expressions such as Eq.~\eqref{eq:flexfactor} in modern piping codes.

\section{Stress Intensification Factors and the Markl fatigue design curve}
\label{sec:sif}
% =============================================================================

Because of the geometric and material discontinuities near fittings, welds and bends, the local stress in these areas is amplified with respect to the nominal beam-theory stress. This amplification is expressed through the \emph{Stress Intensification Factor} (SIF), $i$. Following Markl's original definition, $i$ is not simply a stress concentration factor but a fatigue \emph{severity} factor: it quantifies how much the fatigue endurance of a given detail is degraded relative to a plain, defect-free straight pipe subjected to the same nominal bending stress.

Markl established an empirical fatigue ($S$--$N$) design curve from cyclic bending tests on straight, girth-welded pipe sections, of the form
\begin{equation}
S \cdot N^{n} = m ,
\label{eq:markl}
\end{equation}
where $S = iM/Z$ is the nominal bending stress amplitude multiplied by the SIF, $Z$ the section modulus, $N$ the number of cycles to failure, and $m$, $n$ empirical constants fitted from the test data. Equation~\eqref{eq:markl} is the compact algebraic counterpart of the fatigue-testing programme exemplified in Figure~\ref{fig:markl-photo} and of the final operating equation reproduced in Figure~\ref{fig:markl-equation-frame} (for austenitic stainless steels, $m \approx \SI{1940}{MPa}$ and $n \approx 0.2$).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.72\textwidth]{figures/markl_equation_video_frame.png}
\caption{Final technical frame of the historical video embedded in the source presentation: Markl's operating equation is written as $iM_E/Z \le (f=6/N^{0.2}\le 1.0)(1.25S_c+0.25S_h)$, i.e.\ the thermal-expansion moment range is limited by the fatigue-cycle factor $f$ with an upper cap at 1.0. The accompanying bullets explain that the cap prevents the allowable stress range from increasing any further below about 7000 cycles, and that the $1.25S_c+0.25S_h$ form is intended to prevent ratcheting after deducting the pressure-plus-deadweight share already assumed to consume the hot allowable.}
\label{fig:markl-equation-frame}
\end{figure}

A key methodological point made by Markl is the distinction between two possible references for the SIF:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a \emph{theoretical} reference, based on tests on ideal, notch-free homogeneous material, which yields comparatively high SIFs;
    \item the \emph{as-built product} reference, based on tests on actual commercial, girth-welded pipe (including realistic surface imperfections), which is the reference actually adopted by the design rules.
\end{itemize}
This choice of reference explains why SIFs measured in fatigue tests are systematically lower than those predicted by purely elastic (theoretical) stress-concentration analysis, and it underlies the overall internal consistency of Markl's approach.

In practice, this matters here because the largest SIFs are usually found precisely in the neighbourhood of geometrical discontinuities (elbows, branch connections, fittings, weld transitions), i.e.\ where local plastic strains are also most likely to develop under imposed displacements. The source presentation explicitly uses this observation as the bridge between the classical fatigue/SIF framework and the RCC-MRx abatement framework: the same locations that govern fatigue severity are natural candidates for elastic follow-up and secondary-stress abatement. It also notes that, at high temperature and over long durations, creep and stress relaxation can further modify this redistribution; those time-dependent effects are outside the scope of the present benchmark, which is restricted to monotonic elastic-plastic follow-up.

\section{Elastic follow-up: from local (Neuber) to global (Roche) redistribution}
% =============================================================================

Two conceptually different but often-confused corrections are used to relate an elastically computed stress or strain field to the actual elasto-plastic response of a structure: \emph{Neuber's rule} and the \emph{elastic follow-up effect}. Their essential differences are summarised in Table~\ref{tab:neuber-followup}. The source presentation briefly mentions both $r_M$ and $r_C$ as follow-up-related indicators; only the RCC-MRx factor $r_M$ is developed here because it is the quantity actually used in clauses RB~3643 and RB~3651 and in the benchmark calculations.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{Neuber local correction versus global elastic follow-up.}
\label{tab:neuber-followup}
\begin{tabular}{@{}lll@{}}
\toprule
 & \textbf{Neuber} & \textbf{Elastic follow-up} \\
\midrule
Nature & Local & Global \\
Affected zone & Notch / point & Whole structure \\
Type of phenomenon & Stress concentration & Strain redistribution \\
Role & Corrects a local stress peak & Corrects the overall structural response \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\end{table}

Elastic follow-up describes how a locally yielding (softening) section of a structure is ``followed up'' by the surrounding, stiffer parts, which continue to impose additional deformation on the yielded section because the imposed \emph{overall} displacement must still be accommodated. Depending on how stiff the surrounding structure is relative to the yielding section, the behaviour of that section under a nominally secondary (displacement-controlled) load can range from purely secondary (self-limiting, if the surrounding structure is very flexible and simply relaxes) to effectively primary (if the surrounding structure is very stiff and keeps imposing the full displacement onto the yielded, and therefore weaker, section). Figure~\ref{fig:primary-secondary-paths} gives the mechanical analogy illustrating these two limiting behaviours.

\subsection{The Ramberg--Osgood constitutive law}

To model the elasto-plastic material response, the Ramberg--Osgood law is used in its general textbook form, decomposing the total strain into an elastic and a power-law plastic part,
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon_t = \varepsilon_e + \varepsilon_p = \frac{\sigma}{E} + \left(\frac{\sigma}{K}\right)^{1/n},
\label{eq:RO-generic}
\end{equation}
with $K$ a strength coefficient and $n$ a hardening exponent. The source presentation specialises Eq.~\eqref{eq:RO-generic} to RCC-MRx's own parametrisation of the strength coefficient as a temperature-dependent multiple of the 0.2\% proof stress,
\begin{equation}
K(T) = C_0\,R_{p0.2}(T),
\label{eq:KofT}
\end{equation}
and to the code's own definition of the plastic-strain scale (the leading $0.01$, or $100\times$, factor below reflects the code's percent-strain convention), giving the exact form used in the benchmark computations:
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon(\sigma) = \frac{\sigma}{E} + 0.01\left(\frac{\sigma}{C_0\,R_{p0.2,\mathrm{mean}}}\right)^{1/n_0},
\label{eq:RO}
\end{equation}
with, for 316L(N) stainless steel, $n_0 = 0.1125$ ($1/n_0 = 8.89$) and $C_0 = 1.198$; at the benchmark temperature of \SI{550}{\celsius}, $R_{p0.2} \approx \SI{106}{MPa}$, so that the stress scale $C_0R_{p0.2}$ used in the actual benchmark computations of Eq.~\eqref{eq:RO} is about \SI{127}{MPa}. The temperature dependence therefore enters through $R_{p0.2}(T)$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:KofT}, whereas $C_0$ and $n_0$ retain the same normalised curve shape at all temperatures --- i.e., no change of hardening \emph{shape} with temperature is assumed. A separate sensitivity study reported in the presentation, evaluated at \SI{500}{\celsius} (where $R_{p0.2}\approx\SI{110}{MPa}$, so that the reference parameter set gives $C_0R_{p0.2}\approx\SI{131.8}{MPa}$), compares this reference curve with an alternative fit ($C_0=1.233$, $n_0=0.130$, i.e. $1/n_0=7.69$, $C_0R_{p0.2}\approx\SI{135.6}{MPa}$) calibrated on the same \SI{500}{\celsius} tensile data; the resulting stress--strain curve is only marginally softer than the reference one over the whole strain range plotted, confirming that the choice of hardening parameters is not the dominant source of discrepancy discussed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

Several open questions regarding the domain of validity of this simplified, temperature-invariant hardening shape are noted in the presentation (and are only partially addressed here): the sensitivity to strain rate, to monotonic versus cyclic hardening, to the number of cycles, and to environment (air, PWR primary water, sodium, etc.). Figure~\ref{fig:ro-curves} documents both the temperature family and the parameter sensitivity used to motivate this discussion. For reference, an example comparison at $\SI{0.01}{}$ true strain shows a stress discrepancy of about \SI{4.0}{MPa} ($\sim$\SI{5}{\percent}) between candidate hardening curves \cite{NASAn1930}.

\subsection{Roche's spring-bar analogy}
\label{sec:roche-spring-bar}

R.~Roche's original demonstration of elastic follow-up \cite{Roche1987} uses a simple three-bar analogy:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Bar 1} is subjected to a purely primary load: the elastically computed stress--strain state $(\sigma_{\mathrm{el}},\varepsilon_{\mathrm{el}})$ moves, once plasticity is included, to $(\sigma_1,\varepsilon_1)$ on the Ramberg--Osgood curve, following the classical and potentially dangerous behaviour under primary loading (unbounded deformation for stresses above yield).
    \item \textbf{Bar 2} is subjected to a purely secondary (displacement-controlled) load: with plasticity, the elastic state $(\sigma_{\mathrm{el}},\varepsilon_{\mathrm{el}})$ moves to $(\sigma_2,\varepsilon_2)$, exhibiting the self-limiting character typical of secondary stresses.
    \item \textbf{Bar 3} reproduces Bar 2's geometry and loading but adds a \emph{spring} in series, with an imposed end displacement calibrated so that the resulting \emph{elastic} stress equals that of Bars 1 and 2. Despite representing a nominally purely secondary load case, Bar 3 exhibits behaviour intermediate between that of Bar 1 (primary) and that of Bar 2 (secondary): $(\sigma_3,\varepsilon_3)$ lies between $(\sigma_1,\varepsilon_1)$ and $(\sigma_2,\varepsilon_2)$.
\end{itemize}

This example shows that a nominally secondary stress can, in the presence of elastic follow-up (represented here by the series spring), acquire a partly primary character. A parametric study of the spring stiffness $k_2$ confirms this trend continuously: as $k_2 \to \infty$, Bar~3 tends towards Bar~2's purely secondary behaviour; as $k_2 \to 0$, Bar~3 tends towards Bar~1's primary-like behaviour. Figure~\ref{fig:roche-spring-diagram} shows the conceptual three-bar construction, Figure~\ref{fig:bar3-contour} its FE counterpart, Figure~\ref{fig:roche-spring-sweep} the spring-stiffness sweep extracted from the presentation, and Figure~\ref{fig:roche-example} an original numerical example from Roche. RCC-MRx later formalised this behaviour through the elastic follow-up factor $r_M$ and the abatement coefficient $g$ (Section~\ref{sec:rccmrx}).

\section{The RCC-MRx elastic follow-up factor and abatement coefficient}
\label{sec:rccmrx}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{Energy balance and definition of the elastic follow-up factor}

Before stating the RCC-MRx result, it is useful to make explicit the two independent hypotheses combined in the presentation to obtain it, since they are attributed to two different authors and are easily conflated. The material non-linearity is represented by the Ramberg--Osgood strain decomposition, written here in incremental form relative to a reference elastic state (subscript $0$),
\begin{equation}
\Delta\bar{\bar\varepsilon} = \varphi\,\Delta\bar{\bar\varepsilon}_0 + \Phi(\varphi)\,\Delta\bar{\bar\varepsilon}_0^{P},
\label{eq:kachanov-ro}
\end{equation}
while the loading-path non-linearity is represented by Kachanov's hypothesis of a radially proportional (self-similar) stress redistribution,
\begin{equation}
\Delta\bar{\bar\sigma} = \varphi\,\Delta\bar{\bar\sigma}_0 ,
\label{eq:kachanov-stress}
\end{equation}
with the same scalar multiplier $\varphi$ in both relations, and $\Phi(\varphi)=\varphi^{1/n_0}$ the Ramberg--Osgood exponent function of Eq.~\eqref{eq:RO}. Equation~\eqref{eq:kachanov-stress} -- i.e., that the elasto-plastic stress field remains everywhere collinear with the purely elastic one, only rescaled by $\varphi$ -- is Kachanov's contribution and is the key simplifying assumption that turns a genuinely non-linear redistribution problem into a scalar one; Eq.~\eqref{eq:kachanov-ro} is the Ramberg--Osgood material response evaluated along that self-similar path. Writing the virtual-work statement of the elasto-plastic increment against the elastic reference field, and separating an imposed-displacement contribution $\Delta u$ (secondary) from a thermal/imposed-strain contribution $\Delta\bar{\bar e}$,
\begin{equation}
\varphi\!\int \Delta\bar{\bar\varepsilon}_0{:}\Delta\bar{\bar\sigma}_0\,dv + \Phi(\varphi)\!\int \Delta\bar{\bar\varepsilon}_0^{P}{:}\Delta\bar{\bar\sigma}_0\,dv
= \int \Delta u\,\Delta F_0\,ds + \int \Delta\bar{\bar e}{:}\Delta\bar{\bar\sigma}_0\,dv ,
\label{eq:kachanov-vw}
\end{equation}
and comparing with the energy balance of the purely elastic calculation (same loading, $\varphi=\Phi=1$),
\begin{equation}
\tfrac12\!\int \Delta\bar{\bar\varepsilon}_0{:}\Delta\bar{\bar\sigma}_0\,dv = \tfrac12\!\int \Delta u\,\Delta F_0\,ds + \tfrac12\!\int \Delta\bar{\bar e}{:}\Delta\bar{\bar\sigma}_0\,dv ,
\label{eq:kachanov-elastic-vw}
\end{equation}
subtracting \emph{twice} Eq.~\eqref{eq:kachanov-elastic-vw} (which clears its $\tfrac12$ prefactor) from Eq.~\eqref{eq:kachanov-vw} eliminates the (generally unknown) right-hand-side loading terms and leaves a purely field-integral relation,
\begin{gather}
(\varphi-1)\int \Delta\bar{\bar\varepsilon}_0{:}\Delta\bar{\bar\sigma}_0\,dv + \Phi(\varphi)\int \Delta\bar{\bar\varepsilon}_0^{P}{:}\Delta\bar{\bar\sigma}_0\,dv = 0
\;\Longrightarrow\;
(\varphi-1)\,T + \Phi(\varphi) = 0, \\
\frac1T = \frac{\displaystyle\int \Delta\bar{\bar\varepsilon}_0^{P}{:}\Delta\bar{\bar\sigma}_0\,dv}{\displaystyle\int \Delta\bar{\bar\varepsilon}_0{:}\Delta\bar{\bar\sigma}_0\,dv} ,
\label{eq:kachanov-T}
\end{gather}
which defines the global resilience $T$ purely from the elastic solution, without solving the non-linear problem. Passing to equivalent (von~Mises) stress and strain increments $\Delta\varepsilon^{*}$, $\Delta\sigma^{*}$ as in Eq.~\eqref{eq:roche89}, and using the elastic relation $\Delta\varepsilon_0^{*}=2(1+\nu)\,\Delta\sigma_0^{*}/3E$, Eq.~\eqref{eq:kachanov-T} reduces to the compact result derived in full in Eq.~\eqref{eq:roche2223} of Appendix~\ref{app:roche},
\begin{equation}
\frac{3}{2(1+\nu)}\cdot\frac{E\left(\Delta\varepsilon^{*}-\Delta\varepsilon_0^{*}\right)}{\Delta\sigma_0^{*}-\Delta\sigma^{*}} = \frac{T}{t}-1 ,
\label{eq:kachanov-final}
\end{equation}
i.e. the same elastic follow-up factor $r_M$ recovered below from Roche's total-quantity (rather than incremental) notation; the full step-by-step derivation, including the definition of the local resilience $t$, is reproduced in Appendix~\ref{app:roche}.

RCC-MRx clause RB~3643 formalises the elastic follow-up factor $r_M$ from an energy balance of the purely elastic calculation, following R.~Roche's original derivation (reproduced in full in Appendix~\ref{app:roche}). Let $(\sigma_{\mathrm{el}},\varepsilon_{\mathrm{el}})$ be the elastically computed equivalent stress and strain at the section of interest, and $(\sigma,\varepsilon)$ the actual (elasto-plastic) equivalent stress and strain reached at the same section once the true, non-linear material law (here, Ramberg--Osgood, Eq.~\eqref{eq:RO}) is accounted for, with the loading history assumed radially proportional ($\bar{\bar\sigma} = \varphi\,\bar{\bar\sigma}_0$, i.e. the elasto-plastic stress tensor field remains everywhere proportional to the elastic one through a single scalar $\varphi$). RCC-MRx defines the elastic follow-up factor as
\begin{equation}
r_M \;=\; -\frac{E(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{el}})}{\sigma-\sigma_{\mathrm{el}}} \;=\; \frac{T}{t}-1 \;\ge 0,
\label{eq:rM}
\end{equation}
where $t$ is the \emph{local} resilience (degree of elasticity) of the section under consideration, and $T$ is a \emph{global} resilience computed over the whole structure by weighting every section by its elastic reference stress:
\begin{equation}
T = \frac{\int \sigma_{\mathrm{ref}}^2 A\, ds}{\displaystyle\int \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{ref}}^2 A}{t}\, ds}, \qquad
\frac{1}{t} = \frac{\varepsilon_{M,p}(\sigma_{\mathrm{ref}})}{\sigma_{\mathrm{ref}}/E}, \qquad
\sigma_{\mathrm{ref}}^2 = \left[0.79\,B_2\,\frac{(m)'_R}{Z}\right]^2 + \left[0.87\,\frac{m_1}{Z}\right]^2,
\label{eq:Tglobal}
\end{equation}
with $\varepsilon_{M,p}(\sigma) = 0.01\left[\sigma/\big(C_0\,R_{p0.2,\mathrm{mean}}\big)\right]^{1/n_0}$ the plastic part of the mean Ramberg--Osgood curve (Eq.~\eqref{eq:RO}), and $m_1$, $(m)'_R$ the primary and secondary (resultant) moments at the section, $Z$ its section modulus. Equation~\eqref{eq:Tglobal} is the practical, piping-code form of $T$; its rigorous derivation from the energy balance $\iiint \bar{\bar\sigma}_0:\bar{\bar\varepsilon}\,dV = \iiint \bar{\bar\sigma}_0:\bar{\bar\varepsilon}_0\,dV$ is given in Appendix~\ref{app:roche}, Eqs.~(13)--(14) and (22).

For isotropic materials, the rigorous derivation of Appendix~\ref{app:roche} gives $r_M = \frac{2(1+\nu)}{3}\left(\frac{T}{t}-1\right)$, which for steels ($\nu=0.3$) gives $r_M = 0.867\left(\frac{T}{t}-1\right)$. Conservatively, RCC-MRx takes $\frac{2(1+\nu)}{3}\approx 1$ (absorbing the 3-D correction into a separate stress index of 0.87 applied to pressure and torsional moments), so that the code ultimately uses the simplified form of Eq.~\eqref{eq:rM}, $r_M = T/t - 1$.

The factor $r_M$ has the following limiting behaviour:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $r_M = 0$ ($T=t$): no elastic follow-up; the stress is purely secondary (fully self-limiting).
    \item $r_M \to \infty$ ($T \gg t$): very significant elastic follow-up; the stress behaves as purely primary.
\end{itemize}
Note that a \emph{higher} $r_M$ is treated by the code as a \emph{more conservative}, more primary-like, assumption; taking $r_M(\mathrm{coeff}=1) > r_M(\mathrm{coeff}=0.867)$ is therefore itself a conservative simplification.

In the Roche three-bar analogy, Bar 2 (no spring) corresponds to $r_M=0$, Bar 1 (rigid connection) corresponds to $r_M\to\infty$, and Bar 3 (elastic spring) lies at an intermediate, finite value of $r_M$ that depends on the spring stiffness $k_2$.

Operationally, the presentation summarises the RCC-MRx workflow in four steps: (i) perform a purely elastic piping calculation; (ii) deduce the local plastic strain function $\varepsilon_{M,p}(\sigma_{\mathrm{ref}})$ from the adopted Ramberg--Osgood law; (iii) compute the global resilience $T$ by stress-squared weighting of the elastic reference field over the whole structure; and (iv) retain the governing local value of $r_M$ (and therefore of $g$) at the critical section. Figures~\ref{fig:rm-graphical-overview} and \ref{fig:energy-balance-scheme} summarise these complementary viewpoints: a graphical construction on the constitutive curve and an energy-balance route derived from the elastic FE solution. In other words, the code is intended to recover an elasto-plastic \emph{estimate} from an elastic base calculation rather than to replace full non-linear analysis in all circumstances.

\subsection{Definition of the abatement coefficient}

For a section without internal pressure, RCC-MRx clause RB~3651.1131 defines the abatement coefficient as the ratio $g=\sigma/\sigma_{\mathrm{el}}$ of the true elasto-plastic stress to the elastically computed stress at the same imposed strain. The same result can be written directly from $r_M$ and the Ramberg--Osgood law as
\begin{equation}
g \;=\; \frac{\sigma\,(1+r_M)}{E\,\varepsilon_{M,p}(\sigma) + \sigma\,(1+r_M)} .
\label{eq:gsimple}
\end{equation}
When a primary membrane stress from pressure, $\sigma_P = 0.87\,\dfrac{P D_i}{2h_c}$, is also present at the section (with $\sigma_P<\sigma$), the code generalises Eq.~\eqref{eq:gsimple} to
\begin{equation}
g \;=\; \frac{(\sigma-\sigma_P)(1+r_M)}{E\left[\varepsilon_{M,p}(\sigma)-\varepsilon_{M,p}(\sigma_P)\right] + (\sigma-\sigma_P)(1+r_M)} .
\label{eq:gfull}
\end{equation}
Figure~\ref{fig:rccmrx-official} reproduces the code's own graphical construction of $g$, equivalent to Eqs.~\eqref{eq:gsimple}--\eqref{eq:gfull}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/rccmrx_official_g_figure.png}
\caption{RCC-MRx Figure RB~3651.1132 (reproduced from the code): graphical definition of the abatement coefficient $g$, showing the average Ramberg--Osgood tensile curve, the elastic (slope $E$) and elastic-follow-up (slope $-E/r_M$) construction lines, the primary membrane stress $\sigma_p$ and allowable stress $S^{*}$.}
\label{fig:rccmrx-official}
\end{figure}

The coefficient $g$ is then used to reduce (``abate'') the displacement-dependent moment $m$ (secondary, in the classical sense) into an abated value $g\,m$, which is combined with the primary loads in the assessment of primary-type (P-type) damage through the RCC-MRx CMS1 stress-limitation rule (RB~3651.1131):
\begin{equation}
\overline{s_m+s_b} = \left[\left(D_1\left[\frac{PD_i}{2h_c}\right]\right)^2 + \left\langle D_2/Z,\, M + g\cdot m\right\rangle^2\right]^{0.5} \le S^{*} .
\label{eq:CMS1}
\end{equation}
In this way, the combined effects of elastic follow-up and plasticity are accounted for in the P-type damage check of RCC-MRx, via $\overline{s_m+s_b}$, without requiring the analyst to perform a full elasto-plastic finite-element calculation. The presentation motivates exactly this rule by asking whether secondary loads must be considered when validating primary stresses: the answer is illustrated by re-using the Bar~1/Bar~2/Bar~3 Ramberg--Osgood curve already given in Figure~\ref{fig:roche-spring-sweep} (bottom-right panel) -- since Bar~3's response (points A--D) sits strictly between the purely primary (Bar~1) and purely secondary (Bar~2) limits, a nominally secondary moment can behave partly as a primary one, which is precisely why $g\cdot m$, rather than $m$ alone, is added inside the CMS1 check of Eq.~\eqref{eq:CMS1}.

For the benchmark section reported later in Figure~\ref{fig:hexagon-benchmark-table}, the code output gives $\sigma=\SI{119.59}{MPa}$, $\varepsilon_{M,p}(\sigma)=\SI{0.587}{\percent}$ and $r_M=2.638$. Substituting these values into Eq.~\eqref{eq:gsimple} with $E=\SI{155000}{MPa}$ yields
\[
g = \frac{119.59(1+2.638)}{155000\times0.00587 + 119.59(1+2.638)} = 0.3235,
\]
i.e.\ the reported production-code value $g=0.3237$ to within rounding of the tabulated strain $\varepsilon_{M,p}$. This direct numerical check was used here as an internal verification of the transcribed RCC-MRx formulae.

\section{Elastic analysis and model calibration}
\label{sec:elastic-analysis}

\subsection{Constitutive model}

The elasto-plastic shell analysis uses the von Mises yield criterion with isotropic hardening, and the Ramberg--Osgood law of Eq.~\eqref{eq:RO} calibrated to 316L(N) at \SI{550}{\celsius} (RCC-MRx RB~A3.3S.451), with yielding onset defined at zero offset stress consistent with the code's convention. The material is defined in MSC~Marc by $E=\SI{155000}{MPa}$, $\nu=0.3$, and the von Mises yield condition $\sigma_{\mathrm{VM}} < \sigma_Y$ with
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\mathrm{VM}} := \sqrt{\tfrac32\,s_{ij}s_{ij}} ,
\end{equation}
$s_{ij}$ the deviatoric stress. Hardening is tracked through the equivalent plastic strain
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon_{p,\mathrm{eq}} \;:=\; \sqrt{\tfrac{2}{3}\,\varepsilon^{p}_{ij}\varepsilon^{p}_{ij}} \, ,
\label{eq:eps-p-eq}
\end{equation}
with $\varepsilon^{p}_{ij}$ the plastic strain tensor, consistent with the standard von Mises formulation. Isotropic hardening is imposed as a tabulated function $\sigma_Y(\varepsilon_{p,\mathrm{eq}}) := F(V1)$, with $V1$ the independent (equivalent plastic strain) variable digitised from the Ramberg--Osgood law of Eq.~\eqref{eq:RO} at zero offset stress: the presentation's \emph{plas\_new} table samples this curve over 34 points, $V1\in[0,1.002]$, $F\in[0.6,213.2]\,\mathrm{MPa}$ (dimensionless scale factor $\times 100$), reproduced in Figure~\ref{fig:hardening-plas-new}.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.42\textwidth]{figures/hardening_curve_plas_new.png}

\section{Discussion: why is the RCC-MRx route less conservative?}
\label{sec:discussion}
% =============================================================================

\subsection{First explanation: primary-equivalent stress conversion}

Slide~44 of the source presentation first recalls clause RB~3651.111, titled (in the original French code) \emph{Limitations des contraintes dues \`a la pression} (``Limitation of stresses due to pressure''), which gathers the classical primary-pressure limits
\[
P_m \le S_m(\theta_m), \qquad
P_L \le 1.5\,S_m(\theta_m), \qquad
P_L + P_b \le 1.5\,S_m(\theta_m).
\]
Within that same elastic-analysis framework, the subsequent conversion argument then moves to RB~3651.112, i.e.\ the general primary membrane-plus-bending check used here. RCC-MRx clause RB~3651.112 requires that, for load combinations governed by level-A criteria, the general primary membrane-plus-bending stress additionally satisfies
\begin{equation}
\overline{p_m+p_b} = B_1\,P\left[\frac{D_e}{2h_c}\right] + \left\langle B_2/Z,\,M\right\rangle \le 1.3\,S_m(\theta_m),
\label{eq:pmpb}
\end{equation}
i.e. the abated, displacement-dependent stress must ultimately be re-expressed and re-checked on the strictly primary allowable basis $1.3\,S_m(\theta_m)$, rather than on the CMS1 allowable $S^{*}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:CMS1} used to derive $g$ in Section~\ref{sec:rccmrx}. For the benchmark section considered here, $1.3\,S_m = \SI{100.1}{MPa}$ while $S^{*} = \SI{48.156}{MPa}$, so that the ratio
\begin{equation}
\frac{1.3\,S_m}{S^{*}} = \frac{100.1}{48.156} = 2.079
\end{equation}
is the conversion factor that must be applied when re-scaling the RCC-MRx abatement coefficient and stress from the $S^{*}$ (CMS1) basis onto the $1.3\,S_m$ (primary) basis:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
g_{\mathrm{RCC\text{-}MRx}}^{\mathrm{rabat}} &=
g\cdot\frac{1.3\,S_m}{S^{*}}
= 0.323\times 2.079
= 0.671, \\
(\sigma_m+\sigma_b)^{\mathrm{rabat}} &=
(\sigma_m+\sigma_b)\cdot\frac{1.3\,S_m}{S^{*}}
= \SI{138.23}{MPa}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}
Once this conversion is applied, the RCC-MRx-based primary-equivalent stress ($g^{\mathrm{rabat}}=0.671$, $(\sigma_m+\sigma_b)^{\mathrm{rabat}}=\SI{138.23}{MPa}$) becomes markedly \emph{more} conservative than the elasto-plastic FE reference ($g=0.436$, $\sigma_{\mathrm{VM}}=\SI{86.5}{MPa}$), reversing the apparent non-conservatism identified in Table~\ref{tab:g-comparison}. This confirms that the raw comparison of Table~\ref{tab:g-comparison} is not made on a consistent allowable-stress basis: $g$ and $\sigma_m+\sigma_b$ as computed under the CMS1 rule (RB~3651.1131, allowable $S^{*}$) are not directly comparable to a von Mises stress checked against $1.3\,S_m$, and the code-mandated re-scaling of Eq.~\eqref{eq:pmpb} restores the expected conservative character of the analytical RCC-MRx route.

\subsection{Second explanation: mesh sensitivity of the reference solution}

A complementary explanation emerges from the mesh sensitivity already noted in Table~\ref{tab:elastic-calib}. Two distinct pieces of evidence are available in the source presentation. First, slide~38 contains a native Office chart titled \emph{Mesh convergence analysis}, whose exact data are reproduced in Appendix~\ref{app:mesh-sensitivity} (Table~\ref{tab:mesh-vm-data}). Those four points are the maximum von Mises stresses extracted from the shell calculations, not the $g$ values themselves: the raw stress sequence is \SIlist{105.02;83.86;84.57;86.5}{MPa} for 10, 20, 40 and 80 circumferential elements, respectively, so it is not strictly monotonic. The key observation is that the coarse 10-element mesh is an outlier, whereas the 20-, 40- and 80-element meshes cluster within a much narrower band and approach the retained elasto-plastic reference value of \SI{86.5}{MPa}. Second, the separate NS/NewSolver sensitivity plot reproduced in Figure~\ref{fig:g-mesh-convergence} tracks the derived RCC-MRx abatement coefficient as the discretisation is refined; it shows an overall upward stabilisation trend with small oscillations, rather than a perfectly monotonic increase. Taken together, these two slides indicate that once mesh convergence is properly achieved, the discrepancy between the RCC-MRx analytical route and the elasto-plastic reference is substantially reduced, even before the primary-equivalent stress conversion of Section~\ref{sec:discussion} is applied. This suggests that part of the discrepancy originally observed in Table~\ref{tab:g-comparison} originates from an insufficiently refined shell mesh in the reference elasto-plastic model, rather than from an intrinsic shortcoming of the RCC-MRx $r_M/g$ formulation itself.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.65\textwidth]{figures/g_vs_mesh_convergence.png}
\caption{NS/NewSolver mesh-sensitivity plot extracted from the source presentation (slide~54), showing the derived RCC-MRx abatement coefficient $g$ as a function of discretisation refinement. \label{fig:g-mesh-convergence}
\end{figure}

Both explanations are consistent with one another and are not mutually exclusive: the primary-equivalent stress conversion is a code-mandated, conservatism-restoring step that must always be applied; the mesh-sensitivity finding is a numerical-modelling caveat that must be controlled independently, regardless of which analytical route (RCC-MRx or direct elasto-plastic FE) is used.

\subsection{Analytical validation on a cantilever with imposed end displacement}

To validate the production-code implementation of $r_M$ and $g$ independently of the elbow benchmark, a simple, closed-form case is considered: a straight cantilever beam of length $l$, clamped at one end, subjected to an imposed transverse displacement $\Delta u$ at the free end (Figure~\ref{fig:cantilever-sketch}). In the benchmark slide deck, the imposed displacement is specified explicitly as $\Delta u=\SI{12}{mm}$. In the closed-form derivation below, the curvilinear abscissa $x$ is measured from the free end ($x=0$) towards the clamp ($x=l$), so that the elastic bending moment and the elastic reference stress vary linearly with $x$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figures/cantilever_sketch.png}
\caption{Schematic of the cantilever validation case: a beam of length $l$, clamped at one end and subjected to an imposed transverse displacement $\Delta u$ at the other; for the analytical derivation, $x$ is measured from the free end to the clamped end.}
\label{fig:cantilever-sketch}
\end{figure}

For this configuration, the elastic reference stress is linear in $x$, $\sigma_{\mathrm{ref}}(x) = \omega\,x$ with $\omega = 0.79\,(3EI/l^{3})\,\Delta u/Z$, and the local resilience $1/t(x)$ follows directly from the Ramberg--Osgood plastic strain $\varepsilon_{M,p}$. Carrying out the integrals of Eq.~\eqref{eq:Tglobal} in closed form for this linear stress distribution gives the elastic follow-up factor as an explicit function of position,
\begin{equation}
r(x) \;=\; \frac{T}{t}-1 \;=\; \frac{1+2n_0}{3n_0}\left(\frac{x}{l}\right)^{\frac{1-n_0}{n_0}} - 1 ,
\label{eq:cantilever-rx}
\end{equation}
which is monotonically increasing towards the clamp and is therefore maximised at the clamped end ($x=l$), giving the closed-form bound
\begin{equation}
r_{\max} \;=\; \frac{1-n_0}{3n_0} .
\label{eq:cantilever-rmax}
\end{equation}
For the benchmark material ($n_0=0.1125$), the same closed-form development gives the global resilience quantity $T=0.444$, and Eqs.~\eqref{eq:cantilever-rx}--\eqref{eq:cantilever-rmax} then give $r_{\max}=2.63$. Using the no-pressure RCC-MRx expression for the abatement coefficient on that analytical solution yields $g=0.3118$, i.e.\ essentially the same value as the production-code result $g=0.3116$ reported below. Figures~\ref{fig:cantilever-3d}, \ref{fig:cantilever-vm} and \ref{fig:hexagon-cantilever-table} show, respectively, the FE model, the non-linear stress field and the production-code output used to validate this closed-form result.

\section{Conclusions}
% =============================================================================

\begin{enumerate}
\item The paper provides a consolidated route from the historical background and constitutive assumptions to the practical RCC-MRx definitions of $r_M$ and $g$, supported by the original diagrams, code excerpts and derivation material from the source presentation.
\item The elastic benchmark shows that the beam-element piping model and the MSC~Marc shell model agree well in terms of global flexibility once the elbow flexibility factor is introduced consistently and the boundary conditions are treated carefully.
\item For the benchmark elbow, the direct RCC-MRx output gives $r_M=2.638$ and $g=0.3237$, whereas the non-linear shell reference yields a higher effective abatement level when the comparison is made on a raw section-stress basis.
\item This apparent discrepancy is resolved by two complementary observations: the code requires the abated secondary effect to be expressed in terms of primary stress, and the shell reference solution is also sensitive to mesh density in the elbow ovalisation zone (see also Appendix~\ref{app:mesh-sensitivity}).
\item The additional wall-thickness variants and the cantilever validation case support the robustness of the implementation and show that the analytical elastic follow-up route can be checked independently of the main elbow benchmark.
\item Modern numerical tools make such comparisons much faster to perform, but they also increase the importance of modelling choices and verification steps, especially those related to mesh density, shell formulation and boundary-condition idealisation.
\item The present work remains limited by the adopted boundary conditions, shell formulation, temperature-invariant Ramberg--Osgood shape parameters, and the omission of creep, stress relaxation and pressure effects. Extending the study to include pressure loading, alternative hardening descriptions and further mesh-convergence work is a natural next step.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Standard variational development in a FE analysis}

The local equilibrium equation is $\sigma_{ij,j}+f_i=0$. Admitting a variational displacement field $\delta u$ applied at every material point, $\sigma_{ij,j}\delta u_i + f_i \delta u_i = 0$, and summing over the whole structure,
\begin{equation}
\iiint \sigma_{ij,j}\delta u_i \, dV + \iiint f_i \delta u_i \, dV = 0.
\end{equation}
Using
\[
\sigma_{ij,j}\delta u_i = (\sigma_{ij}\delta u_i)_{,j} - \sigma_{ij}\delta u_{i,j},
\qquad
\iiint(\sigma_{ij}\delta u_i)_{,j}\,dV = \iint \sigma_{ij}n_j\delta u_i\,dS,
\]
the divergence theorem gives
\begin{equation}
\iint \sigma_{ij}n_j\delta u_i\,dS - \iiint \sigma_{ij}\delta u_{i,j}\,dV + \iiint f_i\delta u_i\,dV = 0.
\end{equation}
With $\sigma_{ij}\delta u_{i,j} = \sigma_{ij}\tfrac12(\delta u_{i,j}+\delta u_{j,i}) = \sigma_{ij}\delta\varepsilon_{ij}$,
\begin{equation}
\iint \sigma_{ij}n_j\delta u_i\,dS - \iiint \sigma_{ij}\delta\varepsilon_{ij}\,dV + \iiint f_i\delta u_i\,dV = 0.
\label{eq:roche2}
\end{equation}
Assume an external surface load
\[
W_{\mathrm{ext}}=\iint \sigma_{ij}n_j u_i\,dS,
\]
with variational form
\[
\delta W_{\mathrm{ext}} = \iint \delta\sigma_{ij}n_j u_i\,dS + \iint \sigma_{ij}n_j\delta u_i\,dS.
\]
Likewise, the internal work is
\[
\delta W_{\mathrm{int}} = \delta\iiint \bar{\bar\sigma}:\bar{\bar\varepsilon}\,dV
= \iiint \bar{\bar\sigma}:\delta\bar{\bar\varepsilon}\,dV + \iiint \delta\bar{\bar\sigma}:\bar{\bar\varepsilon}\,dV.
\]
The principle of virtual work, $\delta W_{\mathrm{int}}=\delta W_{\mathrm{ext}}$, then gives
\begin{equation}
\iint \delta\sigma_{ij}n_j u_i\,dS + \iint \sigma_{ij}n_j\delta u_i\,dS = \iiint \sigma_{ij}\delta\varepsilon_{ij}\,dV + \iiint \delta\sigma_{ij}\varepsilon_{ij}\,dV.
\label{eq:roche3}
\end{equation}
From \eqref{eq:roche2}--\eqref{eq:roche3}, in the case $f_i=0$, one obtains the compact equality between external complementary work and the internal stress--strain product,
\begin{equation}
\iint \delta\sigma_{ij}n_j u_i\,dS = \iiint \delta\sigma_{ij}\varepsilon_{ij}\,dV.
\label{eq:roche4}
\end{equation}
Introducing a thermal (or other imposed-strain) contribution $\bar{\bar\epsilon}$, distinct from the mechanical strain $\bar{\bar\varepsilon}$,
\begin{equation}
\iiint \delta\bar{\bar\sigma}:\bar{\bar\varepsilon}\,dV = \iint \delta\bar{\bar\sigma}\,\vec n\,\vec u\,dS - \iiint \delta\bar{\bar\sigma}:\bar{\bar\epsilon}\,dV.
\label{eq:roche6}
\end{equation}
This is the working form used by Roche to derive $r_M$.

\subsection{Radially proportional elasto-plastic stress field}

Roche's method assumes that the elasto-plastic stress distribution remains, at every point, homogeneously proportional to the purely elastic solution, through a single scalar $\varphi$:
\begin{equation}
\bar{\bar\sigma} = \varphi\,\bar{\bar\sigma}_0.
\label{eq:roche7}
\end{equation}
Defining the equivalent (von Mises) stress and strain through Eq.~\eqref{eq:roche89},
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon^{*} = \sqrt{\tfrac23 e_{ij}e_{ij}},\ e_{ij}=\varepsilon_{ij}-\tfrac13\varepsilon_{kk}\delta_{ij}; \qquad
\sigma^{*} = \sqrt{\tfrac32 s_{ij}s_{ij}},\ s_{ij}=\sigma_{ij}-\tfrac13\sigma_{kk}\delta_{ij},
\label{eq:roche89}
\end{equation}
Eq.~\eqref{eq:roche7} implies $\sigma^{*}=\varphi\,\sigma_0^{*}$, so that the elastic part of the equivalent strain, evaluated at the actual stress $\sigma^{*}$, is simply $\varphi$ times its elastic-reference value, $\varepsilon^{*}_{\mathrm{el}}(\sigma^{*})=\varphi\,\varepsilon^{*}_{\mathrm{el}}(\sigma_0^{*})=\varphi\,\varepsilon_0^{*}$ (a direct consequence of Hooke's law being linear in stress). The plastic equivalent strain follows the Ramberg--Osgood law, Eq.~\eqref{eq:RO}:
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon^{*}(\sigma^{*}) = \varphi\,\varepsilon^{*}_{\mathrm{el}}(\sigma_0^{*}) + 0.01\left(\frac{\varphi\sigma_0^{*}}{C_0 R_{p0.2,\mathrm{mean}}}\right)^{1/n_0}
= \varphi\varepsilon_0^{*} + \phi\,\varepsilon_0^{p*}, \qquad \phi=\varphi^{1/n_0},
\label{eq:roche11}
\end{equation}
with $\varepsilon_0^{p*} = 0.01\left(\sigma_0^{*}/(C_0 R_{p0.2,\mathrm{mean}})\right)^{1/n_0}$.

\subsection{Energy balance}

Using Hooke's law $\varepsilon_{0ij} = \tfrac{1+\nu}{E}\sigma_{0ij}-\tfrac{\nu}{E}\sigma_{0kk}\delta_{ij}$, its trace gives $\varepsilon_{0kk} = \tfrac{1-2\nu}{E}\sigma_{0kk}$, so that the deviatoric mechanical strain becomes
\begin{equation}
e_{0ij} = \varepsilon_{0ij}-\tfrac13\varepsilon_{0kk}\delta_{ij}
= \frac{1+\nu}{E}\sigma_{0ij}-\frac{\nu}{E}\sigma_{0kk}\delta_{ij}-\frac13\varepsilon_{0kk}\delta_{ij}
= \frac{1+\nu}{E}\sigma_{0ij}-\frac{\nu}{E}\sigma_{0kk}\delta_{ij}-\frac13\frac{1-2\nu}{E}\sigma_{0kk}\delta_{ij} ,
\end{equation}
which simplifies, using $\sigma_{0ij}-\delta_{ij}\sigma_{0kk}/3=s_{0ij}$, to
\begin{equation}
e_{0ij} = \frac{1+\nu}{E}\left(\sigma_{0ij}-\tfrac13\delta_{ij}\sigma_{0kk}\right) = \frac{1+\nu}{E}\,s_{0ij},
\end{equation}
and hence
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon_0^{*} = \frac{2}{3}\frac{1+\nu}{E}\sigma_0^{*}.
\label{eq:roche21}
\end{equation}
Combining Eqs.~\eqref{eq:roche11} and \eqref{eq:roche21} and defining the \emph{local resilience} $t=\varepsilon_0^{*}/\varepsilon_0^{p*}$, algebraic manipulation gives
\begin{equation}
\frac{3}{2(1+\nu)}\,\frac{E(\varepsilon^{*}-\varepsilon_0^{*})}{\sigma_0^{*}-\sigma^{*}} = \frac{T}{t}-1, \qquad
r_M = \frac{E(\varepsilon^{*}-\varepsilon_0^{*})}{\sigma_0^{*}-\sigma^{*}} \; \Rightarrow\; r_M = \frac{2(1+\nu)}{3}\left(\frac{T}{t}-1\right),
\label{eq:roche2223}
\end{equation}
where the \emph{global resilience} $T$ is obtained, by applying the energy balance of Eq.~\eqref{eq:roche6} to the purely elastic reference state and using Eqs.~\eqref{eq:roche4}, \eqref{eq:roche7}--\eqref{eq:roche11},
\begin{equation}
(\varphi-1)\iiint \sigma_0^{*}\varepsilon_0^{*}\,dV + \phi\iiint \sigma_0^{*}\varepsilon_0^{p*}\,dV = 0
\;\Longrightarrow\;
T \equiv \frac{\iiint \sigma_0^{*}\varepsilon_0^{*}\,dV}{\iiint \sigma_0^{*}\varepsilon_0^{p*}\,dV} = \frac{\iiint {\sigma_0^{*}}^2\,dV}{\displaystyle\iiint \frac{{\sigma_0^{*}}^2}{t}\,dV},
\label{eq:roche1314}
\end{equation}
which, assuming a constant Young's modulus and constant equivalent stress within each piping section, reduces to the practical, section-integrated form used by RCC-MRx, Eq.~\eqref{eq:Tglobal}:
\begin{equation}
T = \frac{\int \sigma_0^{*2} A\,ds}{\displaystyle\int \frac{\sigma_0^{*2}A}{t}\,ds}.
\end{equation}

Equation~\eqref{eq:roche1314} is therefore the continuous-domain counterpart of the piping-code formula of Eq.~\eqref{eq:Tglobal}. For steels ($\nu=0.3$), Eq.~\eqref{eq:roche2223} gives $r_M=0.867(T/t-1)$; the code's simplified, slightly more conservative form $r_M=T/t-1$ (Eq.~\eqref{eq:rM}) is obtained by taking $\tfrac{2(1+\nu)}{3}\approx1$, the associated 3-D correction being reintroduced separately as a 0.87 stress index on pressure and torsional-moment terms (Eq.~\eqref{eq:Tglobal}). This completes, in native equation form, every algebraic step of the internal technical note \cite{RochePipeStressNote} from which this appendix was reconstructed.
