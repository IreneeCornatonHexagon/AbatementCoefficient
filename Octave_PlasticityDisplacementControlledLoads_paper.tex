\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:Intro}
% =============================================================================

Steel pipings are metallic structures (usually pressurized) consisting of straight tubes with hollow cicular cross-sections, assembled by means of welds, fittings (elbows, reducers, tees, etc.) or flanged connections. Due to functional and safety requirements, components such as valves, flow meters, expansion joints and other in-line components might be assembled on piping tubes. The entire structure can be connected to pressurized equipments by means of nozzles and supported by dedicated support structures. Together, these elements constitute a large and complex structure that must be addressed through rigorous engineering analysis.\\

\subsection{Calculation methodology in standard piping analysis}
\label{sec:standardApproach}

Piping stress engineers are interested in the stress state at every material point within pipings that might extend over kilometers. Performing a detailed 3D finite element analysis (FEA) of such structures would be excessively time-consuming and is generally incompatible with the industrial projects' needs. In this context, piping design codes provide rules for the analysis of piping systems, generally based on the assumption of \textbf{linear elastic material behavior} (Hooke's law), with allowable stress limits established to prevent the various failure modes and to ensure an adequate level of safety. \\


Based on the nature of the loading, piping stress analysis distinguishes between \emph{primary} loads (pressure, weight, seismic inertia, etc.) and \emph{secondary} loads (such as thermal expansion). Primary stresses balance the mechanical loads to get equilibrium. Secondary stresses are known for their self-limiting characteristic.\\
Figure~\ref{fig:P_S} shows an example of the two stresses.  \textbf{E} presents the stress state of a material point predicted by an elastic approach.  \textbf{P} and \textbf{S} are stress states responding respectively to primary and secondary loads, considering the elastoplastic law.\\
Primary loads are force-imposed in nature and are balanced by a stress field $\sigma_P=\sigma_E$, regardless of whether the material behavior is described by an elastic or an elastoplastic constitutive law. However, the corresponding elastoplastic strain $\varepsilon_P$ might be significantly larger than the elastically predicted strain  $\varepsilon_E$, under high primary loading conditions, due to plastic flow. As a result, \textbf{excessive deformation} may develop at the structural scale when local plastified zones expand. If the load continues to increase, the stable execessive deformation regime might be lost as structural softenings progressively dominate the global response, eventually lead the structure into the \textbf{plasticity instability} damage mode. Moreover, primary stresses are also responsible for \textbf{buckling} (also known as elastic or elastoplastic instability), due to the presence of unrexable stresses with geometry instabilities.

Secondary loads are displacement-controlled by nature, meaning that the structure is subjected to strain-imposed boundary conditions. As a result, the structure deformation is prescribed and $\varepsilon_S = \varepsilon_E$ regardless of the type of analysis employed. Under sufficiently high secondary loads, localized yielding may occur and plastic flow can develop, leading to elastoplastic stress levels lower than those predicted by a purely elastic analysis: {$\sigma_S \leq \sigma_E$}.  The remainder of the structure, constrained by the imposed-strain boundary conditions, accommodates the local plastic deformation and limits the extent of the plastified region. Secondary stresses are threfore \textbf{self-limiting}. \\Secondary stresses are usually induced by (cyclic) thermal expansion, seismic anchor movements, or differential settlement. The two most significant damage modes are \textbf{fatigue} under cyclic loading and \textbf{progressive deformation}. \\
Piping design codes classify loads according to these two categories and specify allowable stress limits based on the associated failure modes, drawing on both experimental evidence and theoretical considerations. In practice, a linear elastic analysis is used to determine the stress state at each point of the structure (\textbf{E}). The nature of the loading can then be identified, allowing the actual response to be interpreted according to the characteristics of primary (\textbf{P}) or secondary stresses (\textbf{S}) and assessed against the corresponding code compliance criteria. This methodology helps avoid the need for complex nonlinear elastoplastic analyses. \\

The previous paragraphs briefly outlined the main ideas behind piping design codes for addressing the complex structural problems encountered in piping analysis, in particular the stress categorization within a linear approach. A further challenge arises in numerical simulation: even linear 3D finite element analyses (FEA) remain computationally expensive, in terms of model preparation, solution time, and result post-processing. In this context, beam theory plays a key role, providing an efficient means of representing long pipe runs and their associated components while significantly reducing the computational effort required. The classical Euler--Bernoulli hypothesis neglects transverse shear deformation and is only valid for slender members. The Timoshenko beam theory retains transverse shear and remains valid for stockier members as well. Standard nuclear piping codes rely on Timoshenko-type beam elements for the straight and curved segments of the model.  \\
Beam theory was originally developed for straight pipes and must be adapted for curved pipes (bend or elbows). Under bendings, elbows undergoes cross-sectional \textbf{\emph{ovalisation}}: the initial circular cross-section distorts into an oval shape. This ovalisation is a geometrical non-linearity, illustrated by the shell FEA analysis shown in Figure~\ref{fig:ovalisation}, increases the flexibility of the elbow well beyond the prediction of classical (straight) beam theory. The \textbf{\emph{flexibility factor} $k$} is defined as the ratio between the rotation per unit length of the curved component and the rotation per unit length of an equivalent straight pipe under the same bending moment. Von~K\'arm\'an/Hovgaard suggested first approximations to estimate the flexibility factor of elbows, and latter on Beskin introduced simpler reference approximation. In the slide sequence, the latter is presented as the practical formula retained for piping calculations, without an explicit intermediate derivation from the former. The flexibility factor of a smooth, unpressurised elbow is therefore written in the benchmark, in terms of the non-dimensional flexibility characteristic $h$, as
\begin{equation}
h = \frac{t\,R}{r^{2}}, \qquad k = \frac{1.65}{h},
\label{eq:flexfactor}
\end{equation}
where k is the bend flexibility factor, $t$ is the wall thickness, $r$ the mean cross-sectional radius, and $R$ the bend radius. The flexibility factor k will be considered in the stiffness matrix of the Timoshenko beam model.

The configuration consists of two straight pipe segments (10-20 and 30-40) connected by an elbow (20-30). The structure is made of A3.3S material (RCC-MRx Ed. 2022), with an outer diameter of 219.07 mm, a wall thickness of 3.76 mm, a straight-pipe length of 2000 mm for each segment, and an elbow radius of 500 mm. The straight-pipe ends at nodes 10 and 40 are fully anchored. The piping is uniformly heated from an ambient temperature of 20°C to an in-service temperature of 550°C. Only thermal expansion is considered in this analysis, and the material behavior is assumed to be linear elastic. \\



Two independent numerical models are used and cross-checked:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Aspect Nuclear Pipe Stress} (``NS'' solver, PEPS~8.0), a Timoshenko-beam FEA piping model in which the elbow flexibility is represented through the analytical flexibility factor $k$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:flexfactor};
    \item \textbf{MSC~Marc}, a shell finite-element model using bilinear thick-shell elements (four-node, Marc Element~75), in which elbow ovalisation is captured directly by the shell discretisation rather than through an analytical flexibility factor. This element uses bilinear interpolation of coordinates, displacements and rotations, with membrane strains from the displacement field and curvatures from the rotation field; transverse shear strains are evaluated at the mid-edges and interpolated to the Gauss integration points, giving an efficient element that remains accurate in the thin-shell limit while avoiding tying constraints at plate intersections. The two ends of the model are connected to the FE mesh through RBE2 rigid-body elements, as shown in Figure~\ref{fig:rbe2-detail}. RBE2 links can locally generate spurious over-stiffening and stress concentrations; this is considered acceptable here because stresses are not examined at the anchor points themselves (only the elbow region is of interest for stress results), so that the simpler RBE2 connection is preferred over an RBE3 connection.
\end{itemize}

\section{Elastic follow-up}
% =============================================================================
Section \ref{sec:Intro} briefly summarized the approach adopted by most standard piping codes for the assessment of piping stresses. For piping systems used in nuclear power plants, more stringent requirements apply, requiring more sophisticated calculation methodologies, particularly for high-temperature applications.  One of the main differences is the need to consider the so-called \textbf{elastic follow-up}. 

In piping systems, geometrical discontinuities or load-induced deformations (e.g., elbow ovalization) can lead to stress concentrations at specific locations, such as elbows, tees, reducers, or thickness transitions. Under sufficiently high loading, most of the structure may remain in the elastic regime and behave as linear springs, thereby elastically following up and constraining these highly stressed regions where local plastic deformation develops. This phenomenon is referred to as elastic follow-up \cite{RCCMRx} \cite{ASMEIII5} . 

In this section, the elastic follow-up concept will be presented based on the work of Roland L. Roche \cite{Roche1987}, without considering the impacts of creep and stress relaxation.


RCC-MRx is a French nuclear design code entitled \emph{Design and Construction Rules for Mechanical Components of Nuclear Installations: High-Temperature, Research and Fusion Reactors}. In the negligible-creep regime, RCC-MRx RB 3650 considers the interaction between elasticity and plasticity under spring effects and quantifies elastic follow-up using the rules provided in RB 3643.3, following the methodology suggested by Roland L. Roche ( \cite{Roche1987}). Displacement-controlled moments are included in the validation of primary stresses in accordance with the requirements of RB 3651 through an abatement coefficient, $g$, which accounts for the elastic follow-up behavior of the entire structure.

\textbf{The Ramberg--Osgood constitutive law:}

\textcolor{red}{to redo this paragraph on Ramberg-Osgood}

To model the elasto-plastic material response, the Ramberg--Osgood law is used in its general textbook form, decomposing the total strain into an elastic and a power-law plastic part,
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon_t = \varepsilon_e + \varepsilon_p = \frac{\sigma}{E} + \left(\frac{\sigma}{K}\right)^{1/n},
\label{eq:RO-generic}
\end{equation}
with $K$ a strength coefficient and $n$ a hardening exponent. The source presentation specialises Eq.~\eqref{eq:RO-generic} to RCC-MRx's own parametrisation of the strength coefficient as a temperature-dependent multiple of the 0.2\% proof stress,
\begin{equation}
K(T) = C_0\,R_{p0.2}(T),
\label{eq:KofT}
\end{equation}
and to the code's own definition of the plastic-strain scale (the leading $0.01$, or $100\times$, factor below reflects the code's percent-strain convention), giving the exact form used in the benchmark computations:
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon(\sigma) = \frac{\sigma}{E} + 0.01\left(\frac{\sigma}{C_0\,R_{p0.2,\mathrm{mean}}}\right)^{1/n_0},
\label{eq:RO}
\end{equation}
with, for 316L(N) stainless steel, $n_0 = 0.1125$ ($1/n_0 = 8.89$) and $C_0 = 1.198$; at the benchmark temperature of \SI{550}{\celsius}, $R_{p0.2} \approx \SI{106}{MPa}$, so that the stress scale $C_0R_{p0.2}$ used in the actual benchmark computations of Eq.~\eqref{eq:RO} is about \SI{127}{MPa}. The temperature dependence therefore enters through $R_{p0.2}(T)$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:KofT}, whereas $C_0$ and $n_0$ retain the same normalised curve shape at all temperatures --- i.e., no change of hardening \emph{shape} with temperature is assumed. A separate sensitivity study reported in the presentation, evaluated at \SI{500}{\celsius} (where $R_{p0.2}\approx\SI{110}{MPa}$, so that the reference parameter set gives $C_0R_{p0.2}\approx\SI{131.8}{MPa}$), compares this reference curve with an alternative fit ($C_0=1.233$, $n_0=0.130$, i.e. $1/n_0=7.69$, $C_0R_{p0.2}\approx\SI{135.6}{MPa}$) calibrated on the same \SI{500}{\celsius} tensile data; the resulting stress--strain curve is only marginally softer than the reference one over the whole strain range plotted, confirming that the choice of hardening parameters is not the dominant source of discrepancy discussed in Section~\ref{sec:discussion}.

Beam 3 in the above example might be understood as a symbolic representation of a piping structure. The common section acts as the source of plasticity due to stress concentrations occurring at specific weak points within the structure. In reality, these vulnerable locations are not only subjected to direct local loading, but are also influenced by loads transfer through the surrounding parts of the piping system. These less vulnerable regions experience lower stress levels and therefore remain entirely within the elastic regime. In this context, the behavior of the weak links responding to the imposed-secondary loads might be deviated compared to the case when no intermediate structure is present, based on how the intermediate structure (spring) elastically responds to the secondary loads. \\

In the example shown in figure \ref{fig:deviation}, the spring section has a relatively small stiffness compared with the one of the common section, i.e., $k_{spring}=\frac{1}{4}k_{section}$. This stiffness $k_{spring}$ might be viewed as representing a long adjacent piping segment containing several loops, which significantly reduces its overall stiffness. It is also worth noting that the spring section imposes a boundary condition for the common section. Thanks to the surrounding pipings' flexibility, plastic deformation is more likely to develop in the weak zone (the common section). As a result, the position of point \textbf{SP} deviates from its initially predicted location (point \textbf{S}) under the applied secondary load. In other words, in the presence of spring effect and plasticity, the secondary load is no longer strictly displacement-controlled, as assumed in the methodologies adopted by many conventional piping design codes. \\
Moreover, if the spring stiffness is almost zero, plastic deformation can develop almost freely in the weak zone. As a result, point \textbf{SP} converges toward the position of point \textbf{P} (the purely primary-load case), and Beam~3 exhibits essentially the same response as Beam~1. Conversely, if the surrounding structure is very stiff (with an almost infinite stiffness), the weak zone is strongly constrained and plasticity develops in a more controlled manner. In this case, point \textbf{SP} approaches the position of point \textbf{S} (the purely secondary-load case) on the elastoplastic curve, and Beam~3 exhibits essentially the same response as Beam~2. \\

The above remarks are illustrated by points B and D in Figure~\ref{fig:parametric-EFU}. For point B, the spring stiffness is increased to \(k/2\), where \(k\) is the stiffness of the common section. The imposed displacement is increased to 2.435\,mm to achieve the same elastic solution as that predicted for point E. As expected, compared with point SP, the higher spring stiffness causes point B to move closer to the secondary-behavior limit (point S), yielding \(\sigma_B = 90.3\,\text{MPa}\) and \(\varepsilon_B = 0.13\%\). Similarly, for point D, the initial spring stiffness is reduced to \(k/16\), and the imposed displacement is adjusted to 27.591\,mm. As expected, point D moves closer to the primary-behavior limit (point P), yielding \(\sigma_D = 106.7\,\text{MPa}\) and \(\varepsilon_D = 0.283\%\). This parametric study also shows that purely primary behavior is approached as the spring stiffness tends to zero, whereas purely secondary behavior is approached in the limit of infinitely stiff springs.

\subsection{Elastic follow-up factor in RCC-MRx}
\label{sec:rccmrx}
% =============================================================================

This section continues the discussion presented in §\ref{sec:EFF}. With the objective of generalizing the elastic follow-up concept and quantifying the degree of elastic follow-up in a piping system subjected to loading, Roland L. Roche introduced two fundamental assumptions. All details of calculations are provided in Appendix \ref{ApdxA}. \\
The first assumption is the Kachanov hypothesis, which assumes that the elastoplastic stress field (point \textbf{SP}) is proportional to the elastic stress field (point \textbf{E}) through a scalar  $\varphi$, taken to be uniform throughout the entire structure under consideration. See equations \eqref{eq:kachanov-stress} and \eqref{eq:kachanov-stress-equivalent}.\\
The second hypothesis concerns the constitutive law and accounts for material plasticity through the Ramberg--Osgood relation through equations \eqref{eq:Osgood-first-approach}, \eqref{eq:plastic_tensor} and \eqref{eq:Osgood}. \\

\vspace{1cm}
\textbf{First approach:} \\
As a first step, Roland L. Roche considers the axial behavior of a straight pipe under displacement-imposed load. The detailed derivation is provided in Appendix \ref{sec:firstApproach}. Using the principle of virtual work, a relationship is established between the purely elastic solution and the elastoplastic solution through the elastic follow-up factor $r_M$, defined as
\[
r_M=\frac{E\left(\varepsilon(x)-\varepsilon_0(x)\right)}
{\sigma_0(x)-\sigma(x)}.
\]
This definition of $r_M$ corresponds to the inverse of the slope of the segment joining points \textbf{E} and \textbf{SP} in Figure \ref{fig:deviation}.
The energy balance helps to map between point \textbf{E} and point \textbf{SP} by the following formula of rM:
\begin{equation*}
	rM    =\frac{T}{t} -1
\end{equation*}
With local resilience (t) and global resilience (T) defined as below:
\begin{equation*} 
\frac{1}{t}= \frac{ \varepsilon_0^p(x)}{ \varepsilon_0(x)}
\end{equation*} 
\begin{equation*} 
\frac{1}{T}= \frac{ \int_{x=0}^{x=L} \sigma_0(x) \varepsilon_0^p(x) A(x) \, dx }{  \int_{x=0}^{x=L} \sigma_0(x) \varepsilon_0(x) A(x) \, dx }
\end{equation*} 
or 
\begin{equation*} 
\frac{1}{T}=\frac{ \int_{x=0}^{x=L} \frac{1}{t} \sigma_0^2(x) A(x) \, dx}{\int_{x=0}^{x=L} \sigma_0^2(x) A(x) \, dx}     \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{(if E is homogeneous in the structure)}
\end{equation*} 

Local resilience and global resilience are indicators of the inverse degree of plasticity present in a structure, at the local and global scales, respectively. Of particular interest is the fact that both \(t\) and \(T\) are determined solely from the results of a linear elastic analysis (point \textbf{E}). In other words, determining \(r_M\) requires only a linear elastic analysis together with knowledge of the material's plastic constitutive law.

Knowing \(r_M\), which corresponds to the inverse of the slope of the EFU segment (see Figure \ref{fig:deviation}), and using the Ramberg--Osgood relation implicitly defines a system of two equations. Solving this system makes it possible to determine the position of point \textbf{SP} from the elastic solution represented by point \textbf{E}. The detailed derivation is provided in Appendix \ref{application_roche}.

Moreover, the elastic follow-up factor \(r_M\) provides a means of quantifying the degree of elastic follow-up in a generalized piping system without explicitly considering spring stiffness. The higher the value of \(r_M\), the greater the degree of elastic follow-up present in the structure. The parametric study of the spring effect presented in Figure \ref{fig:parametric-EFU} is revisited in Appendix \ref{application_roche}, this time using \(r_M\) as the governing parameter. The results demonstrate that, for the simple case of a straight pipe with a variable cross-section, the objective outlined at the end of §\ref{sec:EFF} is indeed achieved.

\vspace{1cm}
\textbf{Second approach:} \\
As a second step, Roland L. Roche extends the derivation to a general piping system. In addition to displacement-controlled loading, thermal expansion effects are also taken into account. The details of the derivation are provided in Appendix \ref{sec:secondApproach}. The uniaxial Ramberg--Osgood law is generalized to the tensorial framework through Equation \eqref{eq:plastic_tensor}:
\begin{align*}
\bar{\bar\varepsilon}^p &= \phi(\varphi)\,\bar{\bar\varepsilon}_0^p
\end{align*}
To remain consistent with the stress--strain representation of Figure \ref{fig:deviation}, the tensorial formulation must then be recast in terms of equivalent stress and equivalent strain quantities. Following this conversion, the same procedure as in the first approach is applied. The elastic follow-up factor and the associated quantities are obtained as follows:
\begin{equation*}
r_M=\frac{E\left(\varepsilon^*-\varepsilon_0^*\right)}
{\sigma_0^*-\sigma^*}
\end{equation*}

\begin{equation*}
r_M = 0.867\left(\frac{T}{t}-1\right)
< \frac{T}{t}-1
\end{equation*}

\begin{equation*}
\frac{1}{t}=
\frac{\varepsilon_0^{p*}}
{\varepsilon_0^*}
\end{equation*}

\begin{equation*}
\frac{1}{T}
=
\frac{\iiint_V \sigma_0^* \varepsilon_0^{p*}\,dV}
{\iiint_V \sigma_0^* \varepsilon_0^*\,dV}
=
\frac{\iiint_V \frac{1}{t}\sigma_0^{*2}\,dV}
{\iiint_V \sigma_0^{*2}\,dV}
\end{equation*}

Once again, a relationship very similar to that obtained in the first approach is found for \(r_M\), differing only by the factor 0.867. However, the formulation is now generalized to arbitrary piping systems and accounts for multiaxial stress states, displacement-controlled loading, and thermal expansion effects.

\vspace{1cm}
\textbf{Third approach:} \\
Finally, in a third step, the method developed by Roland L. Roche is generalized by removing the need for the tensorial Ramberg--Osgood assumption and relying solely on equivalent stress--strain relations:
\begin{equation*}
\varepsilon^* = \frac{\sigma^*}{E} + \left(\frac{\sigma^*}{K}\right)^{1/n}
\end{equation*}
Following the same methodology as Roland L. Roche, the following relationships are obtained:
\begin{equation*}
r_M=\frac{E\left(\varepsilon^*-\varepsilon_0^*\right)}
{\sigma_0^*-\sigma^*}
\end{equation*}
\begin{equation*}
r_M <
\frac{E\left(\varepsilon^*-\varepsilon_0^*\right)}
{0.867\,\sigma_0^*-\sigma^*}
\end{equation*}
or equivalently,
\begin{equation}
r_M < \frac{T}{t}-1
\label{def:EFU-3rd}
\end{equation}
where \(T\) and \(t\) are defined exactly as in the second approach.

Compared with the method of Roland L. Roche, the resulting expression is no longer an equality but an upper bound on the elastic follow-up factor. Nevertheless, it retains the same interpretation and relies on the same resilience indicators \(t\) and \(T\).


\vspace{1cm}

\textbf{Elastic follow-up factor in RCC-MRx RB3650:} \\
RCC-MRx clause RB~3643 formalises the elastic follow-up factor $r_M$ from an energy balance of the purely elastic calculation, following R.~Roche's original derivation (reproduced in full in Appendix \ref{sec:firstApproach} and Appendix \ref{sec:secondApproach}, extended in Appendix \ref{sec:thirdApproach}). Let $(\sigma_{\mathrm{el}},\varepsilon_{\mathrm{el}})$ be the elastically computed equivalent stress and strain at the section of interest, and $(\sigma,\varepsilon)$ the actual (elasto-plastic) equivalent stress and strain reached at the same section once the true, non-linear material law (here, Ramberg--Osgood, Eq.~\eqref{eq:RO}) is accounted for, with the loading history assumed radially proportional ($\bar{\bar\sigma} = \varphi\,\bar{\bar\sigma}_0$, i.e. the elasto-plastic stress tensor field remains everywhere proportional to the elastic one through a single scalar $\varphi$). RCC-MRx defines the elastic follow-up factor as
\begin{equation}
r_M \;=\; -\frac{E(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{\mathrm{el}})}{\sigma-\sigma_{\mathrm{el}}} \;=\; \frac{T}{t}-1 \;\ge 0,
\label{eq:rM}
\end{equation}
where $t$ is the \emph{local} resilience (degree of elasticity) of the section under consideration, and $T$ is a \emph{global} resilience computed over the whole structure by weighting every section by its elastic reference stress:
\begin{equation}
T = \frac{\int \sigma_{\mathrm{ref}}^2 A\, ds}{\displaystyle\int \frac{\sigma_{\mathrm{ref}}^2 A}{t}\, ds}, \qquad
\frac{1}{t} = \frac{\varepsilon_{M,p}(\sigma_{\mathrm{ref}})}{\sigma_{\mathrm{ref}}/E}, \qquad
\sigma_{\mathrm{ref}}^2 = \left[0.79\,B_2\,\frac{(m)'_R}{Z}\right]^2 + \left[0.87\,\frac{m_1}{Z}\right]^2,
\label{eq:Tglobal}
\end{equation}
with $\varepsilon_{M,p}(\sigma) = 0.01\left[\sigma/\big(C_0\,R_{p0.2,\mathrm{mean}}\big)\right]^{1/n_0}$ the plastic part of the mean Ramberg--Osgood curve (Eq.~\eqref{eq:RO}), and $m_1$, $(m)'_R$ the primary and secondary (resultant) moments at the section, $Z$ its section modulus. 

The solution adopted by RCC-MRx encompasses all three approaches presented above.

\subsection{Abatement coefficient g in RCC-MRx}
Up to this point, we have reviewed how standard piping codes address complex structural behavior through linear analysis supplemented by stress categorization (§\ref{sec:standardApproach}), examined the limitations of the secondary stress concept in the presence of elastic follow-up (§\ref{sec:EFF}), and presented the quantification of elastic follow-up through the RCC-MRx methodology (§\ref{sec:rccmrx}).


This observation raises an important question, particularly in light of the results presented in Figure \ref{fig:deviation}. Due to the presence of elastic follow-up, loads traditionally classified as secondary exhibit a deviation from the expected secondary-stress behavior and partially resemble the behavior of primary loads. This observation highlights the need to account for the contribution of secondary loads when assessing the acceptability of primary stresses.


\vspace{1cm}


\textbf{Type P damages and sm+sb stresses in RCC-MRx piping analysis:} \\
According to RCC-MRx RB 3600, type-P damage mechanisms include excessive deformation, plastic instability, fracture, and both elastic and elastoplastic instabilities. The design rules intended to prevent these failure modes are provided in RCC-MRx RB 3650. Among the type-P assessments required by the code is the verification of the \(\overline{s_m+s_b}\) stress category:

\begin{equation}
\overline{s_m+s_b} = \left[\left(D_1\left[\frac{PD_i}{2h_c}\right]\right)^2 + \left\langle D_2/Z,\, M + g\cdot m\right\rangle^2\right]^{0.5} \le S^{*} .
\label{eq:CMS1}
\end{equation}
%
%
%\begin{figure}[htbp]
%\centering
%\includegraphics[width=0.4\textwidth]{figures/sm+sb.png}
%\caption{\(\overline{s_m+s_b}\) stress validation in RCC-MRx RB 3651.1131}
%\label{fig:sm+sb}
%\end{figure}

As expected, type-P damage mechanisms are primarily associated with primary stresses. Consequently, the verification of \(\overline{s_m+s_b}\) should, in principle, involve only loads exhibiting a primary character.

However, equation \eqref{eq:CMS1} shows that, in addition to pressure \(P\) and displacement-controlled moments \(M\), the stress classification also contains the term \(m\), which represents displacement-controlled moments. The presence of this contribution is noteworthy and directly relates to the question raised earlier in this section. Indeed, RCC-MRx incorporates the effect of displacement-controlled loads in the \(\overline{s_m+s_b}\) assessment, thereby acknowledging that such loads may exhibit a partial primary character when elastic follow-up is present.

This approach is consistent with the observations discussed previously: elastic follow-up causes displacement-controlled loads to deviate from ideal secondary behavior and to contribute, at least partially, to the mechanisms associated with type-P damage.

\vspace{1cm}
\textbf{Abatement coefficient g} \\
As shown in equation~\eqref{eq:CMS1}, displacement-controlled moments are accounted for in the type-P \(s_m+s_b\) assessment through the abatement coefficient \(g\), defined as 
\begin{equation} 
g \;=\; \frac{(\sigma-\sigma_P)(1+r_M)} {E\left[\varepsilon_{M,p}(\sigma)-\varepsilon_{M,p}(\sigma_P)\right] + (\sigma-\sigma_P)(1+r_M)} . 
\label{eq:gfull}
\end{equation} 
Here, \(\varepsilon_{M,p}\) denotes the plastic strain predicted by the Ramberg--Osgood relation. The contribution of the primary pressure stress is removed by introducing \(\sigma_P = 0.87\,\dfrac{P D_i}{2h_c}\) (required to be lower than $\sigma$), which is subtracted from the total stress in the above expression.

An important remark is that RCC-MRx requires the use of the maximum elastic follow-up factor \(r_M\) within the entire structure. This requirement is consistent with the first assumption of Roland L. Roche's method, which assumes a constant proportionality factor \(\varphi\) between the elastoplastic and elastic stress fields (Equation~\eqref{eq:kachanov-stress}). It is also consistent with the objective of the \(s_m+s_b\) assessment, which is intended to prevent elastic and elastoplastic instabilities, both of which are global failure modes governed by the overall structural response.



\vspace{1cm}
\textbf{How to interprete the formula of abatement coefficient g  ?} \\
In a first step, the right-hand side of Equation~\eqref{eq:gfull} is analyzed without considering the specific procedure given in RCC-MRx RB 3651.1132 (let's say the g coefficient is not yet introduced at this stage). Instead, we simply consider an elastoplastic stress \(\sigma\) and its associated elastic solution \(\sigma_{el}\). The elastic follow-up factor is recalled as \[ r_M = -\frac{E\left(\varepsilon-\varepsilon_{el}\right)} {\sigma-\sigma_{el}}. \]
The right-hand side of Equation~\eqref{eq:gfull} can then be rewritten as
\begin{equation*}
\frac{(\sigma-\sigma_P)(1+r_M)} {E\left[\varepsilon_{M,p}(\sigma)-\varepsilon_{M,p}(\sigma_P)\right] +(\sigma-\sigma_P)(1+r_M)} \geq \frac{(\sigma-\sigma_P)(1+r_M)} {E\,\varepsilon_{M,p}(\sigma) +(\sigma-\sigma_P)(1+r_M)} = \frac{\sigma-\sigma_P} {E\,\dfrac{\varepsilon_{M,p}(\sigma)}{1+r_M} +\sigma-\sigma_P} = \frac{\sigma-\sigma_P} {\sigma_{el}-\sigma_P}
\end{equation*}
($"="$ is obtained only in condition that $\sigma_P=0$) \\
So, in essence, the formula in the right-hand-side of equation \eqref{eq:gfull} is used to derive the ratio between the elastoplastic stress and the elastic stress, without considering the primary pressure stress, in a conservative approach.

Now, to satisfy the Kachanov hypothesis, a unique elastic follow-up factor must be considered throughout the structure. To obtain a conservative result, the maximum elastic follow-up factor of the entire structure is used, i.e., \(r_M=r_{M,\max}\). The above inequality can then be developed as follows:
\begin{equation*}
\frac{\sigma-\sigma_P} {\sigma_{el}-\sigma_P}  \leq     \frac{(\sigma-\sigma_P)(1+r_{M,max})} {E\left[\varepsilon_{M,p}(\sigma)-\varepsilon_{M,p}(\sigma_P)\right] +(\sigma-\sigma_P)(1+r_{M,max})}
\end{equation*}
Finally, the displacement-controlled moments \(m\) must be multiplied by the factor $\frac{\sigma-\sigma_P}{\sigma_{el}-\sigma_P}$ in order to obtain the displacement-controlled moment corresponding to the elastoplastic analysis (see the term $g.m$ in equation \eqref{eq:gfull})\\


In practice, if an elastoplastic stress \(\sigma\) is used to estimate the upper bound of the ratio \[ \frac{\sigma-\sigma_P}{\sigma_{el}-\sigma_P}, \] a difficulty remains: elastoplastic analyses are generally not intended to be performed in the context of routine piping design. Therefore, an alternative method is required to evaluate this ratio.
To estimate the abatement coefficient \(g\), RCC-MRx adopts the following expression for \(\sigma\):
\begin{equation*} 
\sigma = 2\,S^{*}\,\left[\frac{(R_{p0.2})_{moy}}{(R_{p0.2})_{\min}}\right]
\end{equation*}
with
\begin{equation*} 
S^{*} = \alpha \left[ 0.426\,(R_{p0.2})_{\min}(\theta_m) + 0.032\,(R_m)_{\min}(\theta_m) \right]
\end{equation*}
From a methodological standpoint, the code assumes a material stress state corresponding to a limiting, or target, stress level associated with the onset of elastic or elastoplastic instability.


The complete abatement procedure defined in RCC-MRx, for monotonic loads, is summarized in Figure~RB~3651.1132.
\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/rccmrx_official_g_figure.png}
\caption{RCC-MRx Figure RB~3651.1132 - the abatement is realized based on a target stress resulting in the elastic/elastoplastic instability limit }
\label{fig:rccmrx-official}
\end{figure}

With this calculation procedure, only elastic analyses are required to evaluate both the elastic follow-up factor \(r_M\) and the abatement coefficient \(g\).

\subsection{Elastoplastic large-strain FEA analysis vs PEPS model}


\textcolor{red}{Use the same model as the elastic benchmark + plasticity}


The elasto-plastic shell analysis uses the von Mises yield criterion with isotropic hardening, and the Ramberg--Osgood law of Eq.~\eqref{eq:RO} calibrated to 316L(N) at \SI{550}{\celsius} (RCC-MRx RB~A3.3S.451), with yielding onset defined at zero offset stress consistent with the code's convention. The material is defined in MSC~Marc by $E=\SI{155000}{MPa}$, $\nu=0.3$, and the von Mises yield condition $\sigma_{\mathrm{VM}} < \sigma_Y$ with
\begin{equation}
\sigma_{\mathrm{VM}} := \sqrt{\tfrac32\,s_{ij}s_{ij}} ,
\end{equation}
$s_{ij}$ the deviatoric stress. Hardening is tracked through the equivalent plastic strain
\begin{equation}

\label{eq:eps-p-eq}
\end{equation}
with $\varepsilon^{p}_{ij}$ the plastic strain tensor, consistent with the standard von Mises formulation. \underline{\textbf{Discussion: why is the RCC-MRx route less conservative?}}


Slide~44 of the source presentation first recalls clause RB~3651.111, titled (in the original French code) \emph{Limitations des contraintes dues \`a la pression} (``Limitation of stresses due to pressure''), which gathers the classical primary-pressure limits
\[
P_m \le S_m(\theta_m), \qquad
P_L \le 1.5\,S_m(\theta_m), \qquad
P_L + P_b \le 1.5\,S_m(\theta_m).
\]
Within that same elastic-analysis framework, the subsequent conversion argument then moves to RB~3651.112, i.e.\ the general primary membrane-plus-bending check used here. RCC-MRx clause RB~3651.112 requires that, for load combinations governed by level-A criteria, the general primary membrane-plus-bending stress additionally satisfies
\begin{equation}
\overline{p_m+p_b} = B_1\,P\left[\frac{D_e}{2h_c}\right] + \left\langle B_2/Z,\,M\right\rangle \le 1.3\,S_m(\theta_m),
\label{eq:pmpb}
\end{equation}
i.e. the abated, displacement-dependent stress must ultimately be re-expressed and re-checked on the strictly primary allowable basis $1.3\,S_m(\theta_m)$, rather than on the CMS1 allowable $S^{*}$ of Eq.~\eqref{eq:CMS1} used to derive $g$ in Section~\ref{sec:rccmrx}. For the benchmark section considered here, $1.3\,S_m = \SI{100.1}{MPa}$ while $S^{*} = \SI{48.156}{MPa}$, so that the ratio
\begin{equation}
\frac{1.3\,S_m}{S^{*}} = \frac{100.1}{48.156} = 2.079
\end{equation}
is the conversion factor that must be applied when re-scaling the RCC-MRx abatement coefficient and stress from the $S^{*}$ (CMS1) basis onto the $1.3\,S_m$ (primary) basis:
\begin{equation}
\begin{aligned}
g_{\mathrm{RCC\text{-}MRx}}^{\mathrm{rabat}} &=
g\cdot\frac{1.3\,S_m}{S^{*}}
= 0.323\times 2.079
= 0.671, \\
(\sigma_m+\sigma_b)^{\mathrm{rabat}} &=
(\sigma_m+\sigma_b)\cdot\frac{1.3\,S_m}{S^{*}}
= \SI{138.23}{MPa}.
\end{aligned}
\end{equation}

\textcolor{red}{Let's review the above paragraph to see if it's appropriate to keep it in the article} \\



Once this conversion is applied, the RCC-MRx-based primary-equivalent stress ($g^{\mathrm{rabat}}=0.671$, $(\sigma_m+\sigma_b)^{\mathrm{rabat}}=\SI{138.23}{MPa}$) becomes markedly \emph{more} conservative than the elasto-plastic FE reference ($g=0.436$, $\sigma_{\mathrm{VM}}=\SI{86.5}{MPa}$), reversing the apparent non-conservatism identified in Table~\ref{tab:g-comparison}. This confirms that the raw comparison of Table~\ref{tab:g-comparison} is not made on a consistent allowable-stress basis: $g$ and $\sigma_m+\sigma_b$ as computed under the CMS1 rule (RB~3651.1131, allowable $S^{*}$) are not directly comparable to a von Mises stress checked against $1.3\,S_m$, and the code-mandated re-scaling of Eq.~\eqref{eq:pmpb} restores the expected conservative character of the analytical RCC-MRx route. \\

\vspace{20mm}



A complementary explanation emerges from the mesh sensitivity already noted in Table~\ref{tab:elastic-calib}. Two distinct pieces of evidence are available in the source presentation. First, slide~38 contains a native Office chart titled \emph{Mesh convergence analysis}, whose exact data are reproduced in Appendix~\ref{app:mesh-sensitivity} (Table~\ref{tab:mesh-vm-data}). Those four points are the maximum von Mises stresses extracted from the shell calculations, not the $g$ values themselves: the raw stress sequence is \SIlist{105.02;83.86;84.57;86.5}{MPa} for 10, 20, 40 and 80 circumferential elements, respectively, so it is not strictly monotonic. The key observation is that the coarse 10-element mesh is an outlier, whereas the 20-, 40- and 80-element meshes cluster within a much narrower band and approach the retained elasto-plastic reference value of \SI{86.5}{MPa}. Second, the separate NS/NewSolver sensitivity plot reproduced in Figure~\ref{fig:g-mesh-convergence} tracks the derived RCC-MRx abatement coefficient as the discretisation is refined; it shows an overall upward stabilisation trend with small oscillations, rather than a perfectly monotonic increase. Taken together, these two slides indicate that once mesh convergence is properly achieved, the discrepancy between the RCC-MRx analytical route and the elasto-plastic reference is substantially reduced, even before the primary-equivalent stress conversion of Section~\ref{sec:discussion} is applied. This suggests that part of the discrepancy originally observed in Table~\ref{tab:g-comparison} originates from an insufficiently refined shell mesh in the reference elasto-plastic model, rather than from an intrinsic shortcoming of the RCC-MRx $r_M/g$ formulation itself.




Both explanations are consistent with one another and are not mutually exclusive: the primary-equivalent stress conversion is a code-mandated, conservatism-restoring step that must always be applied; the mesh-sensitivity finding is a numerical-modelling caveat that must be controlled independently, regardless of which analytical route (RCC-MRx or direct elasto-plastic FE) is used.

\section{Conclusions}
% =============================================================================

\textcolor{red}{to redo these contents}


\begin{enumerate}
\item The paper provides a consolidated route from the historical background and constitutive assumptions to the practical RCC-MRx definitions of $r_M$ and $g$, supported by the original diagrams, code excerpts and derivation material from the source presentation.
\item The elastic benchmark shows that the beam-element piping model and the MSC~Marc shell model agree well in terms of global flexibility once the elbow flexibility factor is introduced consistently and the boundary conditions are treated carefully.
\item For the benchmark elbow, the direct RCC-MRx output gives $r_M=2.638$ and $g=0.3237$, whereas the non-linear shell reference yields a higher effective abatement level when the comparison is made on a raw section-stress basis.
\item This apparent discrepancy is resolved by two complementary observations: the code requires the abated secondary effect to be expressed in terms of primary stress, and the shell reference solution is also sensitive to mesh density in the elbow ovalisation zone (see also Appendix~\ref{app:mesh-sensitivity}).
\item The additional wall-thickness variants and the cantilever validation case support the robustness of the implementation and show that the analytical elastic follow-up route can be checked independently of the main elbow benchmark.
\item Modern numerical tools make such comparisons much faster to perform, but they also increase the importance of modelling choices and verification steps, especially those related to mesh density, shell formulation and boundary-condition idealisation.
\item The present work remains limited by the adopted boundary conditions, shell formulation, temperature-invariant Ramberg--Osgood shape parameters, and the omission of creep, stress relaxation and pressure effects. Extending the study to include pressure loading, alternative hardening descriptions and further mesh-convergence work is a natural next step.



\item Several open questions regarding the domain of validity of this simplified, temperature-invariant hardening shape are noted in the presentation (and are only partially addressed here): the sensitivity to strain rate, to monotonic versus cyclic hardening, to the number of cycles, and to environment (air, PWR primary water, sodium, etc.). \section{Definition of elastic follow-up factor}
\label{ApdxA}




The first assumption pertains to loading-path nonlinearity and is based on Kachanov's hypothesis of a radially proportional (self-similar) stress redistribution:
\begin{equation}
\bar{\bar\sigma} = \varphi\,\bar{\bar\sigma}_0 
\label{eq:kachanov-stress}
\end{equation}


Equation ~\eqref{eq:kachanov-stress} might also be expressed for the equivalent stresses:
\begin{align} 
\sigma^* =\varphi \sigma_0^* 
\label{eq:kachanov-stress-equivalent}
\end{align}





$(\Delta\bar{\bar{\varepsilon}}_0, \Delta\bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0)$ denotes the strain--stress tensor state of the material point obtained from a purely elastic analysis. $\varepsilon_0^*$ and $\sigma_0^*$ are the corresponding equivalent elastic strain and stress, respectively (schematically represented by point \textbf{E} in Figure~\ref{fig:deviation}). See appendix \ref{sec:equivalent_sigma_epsilon_eq} for further details.

$(\Delta\bar{\bar{\varepsilon}}, \Delta\bar{\bar{\sigma}})$ denotes the strain--stress tensor state of the material point obtained from an elastoplastic analysis. $\varepsilon^*$ and $\sigma^*$ are the corresponding equivalent elastoplastic strain and stress, respectively (schematically represented by point \textbf{SP} in Figure~\ref{fig:deviation}).

\begin{equation}
\varepsilon_{axial} = \frac{\sigma_{axial}}{E} + \left(\frac{\sigma_{axial}}{K}\right)^{1/n}
\label{eq:Osgood_code}
\end{equation}

\subsection{First approach: Ramberg-Osgood relation under uniaxial configurations}
\label{sec:firstApproach}

In this first approach, we consider only the axial deformation of the beam under an imposed displacement \textbf{u}. All stress and strain tensors are represented by their corresponding axial scalar quantities: \textbf{SP}($\varepsilon$, $\sigma$) and \textbf{E}($\varepsilon_0$, $\sigma_0$).

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figures/FirstApproach.png}
\caption{A beam is axially loaded by a secodary load-\textcolor{red}{to adap the figure: cycle displacement deltaU is out scope}}
\label{fig:firstApproach}
\end{figure}

For uniaxial loads, the RCC-MRx Ramberg-Osgood formula can be directly applied:
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon(x) = \frac{\sigma(x)}{E}   + \left(\frac{\sigma(x)}{K}\right)^{1/n}
\label{eq:Osgood-first-approach}
\end{equation}

The Khachanov assumption is simply $\sigma(x)=\varphi \sigma_0(x)$. The Ramberg--Osgood relation is rewritten as follows:
\begin{align} 
\varepsilon(x) = \frac{\varphi \sigma_0(x)}{E} + \left(\frac{\varphi \sigma_0(x)}{K}\right)^{1/n}  = \varphi \varepsilon_0(x)  + \phi \varepsilon_0^{p}(x)
\label{eq:Osgood_modified-C}
\end{align}

where $\varepsilon_0^{p}$ is the equivalent plastic strain obtained from the Ramberg--Osgood law using the elastic stress $\sigma_0$, and $\phi=\varphi^{n}$. \\


Now, let's analyze the structure using an elastoplastic approach (point \textbf{SP}):
\begin{equation*}
\int_{x=0}^{x=L} \delta\sigma(x) \varepsilon(x) A(x) \,dx = \delta\sigma(L)A(L)u 
\end{equation*}

Using relation~\eqref{eq:kachanov-stress-equivalent} and introducing the variation of the unknown field, $\delta\varphi$, yields 
\begin{equation*} 
\int_{x=0}^{x=L} (\delta \varphi) \sigma_0(x) \varepsilon(x) A(x) \,dx =(\delta \varphi)\sigma_0(L) A(L) u 
\end{equation*}

\begin{equation} 
\int_{x=0}^{x=L} \sigma_0(x) \varepsilon(x) A(x) \,dx =\sigma_0(L) A(L) u 
\label{eq:EnergyBalance-elastoplastic-A}
\end{equation}


For the elastic solution (point \textbf{E}), consider the same secondary loads \textbf{u}. The principle of virtual work gives 
\begin{equation} 
\frac{1}{2}\int_{x=0}^{x=L} \sigma_0(x)  \varepsilon_0(x) A(x) \, dx = \frac{1}{2}\sigma_0(L) A(L) u 
\label{eq:EnergyBalance-elastic-B}
\end{equation} 

The energy balances for the elastic solution (Equation~\eqref{eq:EnergyBalance-elastic-B}) and the elastoplastic solution (Equation~\eqref{eq:EnergyBalance-elastoplastic-A}) yield \begin{flalign*} 
\int_{x=0}^{x=L} \sigma_0(x) \varepsilon(x) A(x) \,dx &=\int_{x=0}^{x=L} \sigma_0(x)  \varepsilon_0(x) A(x) \, dx
\end{flalign*} 

Substituting Equations~\eqref{eq:kachanov-stress} and ~\eqref{eq:Osgood_modified-C} into the above expression yields 
\begin{flalign*} 
\int_{x=0}^{x=L} \sigma_0(x) \left( \varphi(x) \varepsilon_0(x)  + \phi \varepsilon_0^{p}(x) \right) A(x) \,dx &=\int_{x=0}^{x=L} \sigma_0(x)  \varepsilon_0(x) A(x) \, dx
\end{flalign*} 
\begin{flalign} 
(\varphi-1) \int_{x=0}^{x=L} \sigma_0(x) \varepsilon_0(x) A(x) \, dx + \phi \int_{x=0}^{x=L} \sigma_0(x) \varepsilon_0^p(x) A(x)\, dx &=0
\label{eq:phiiT}
\end{flalign} 


Defining the local resilience t and global resilience T as below:
\begin{equation*} 
\frac{1}{t}= \frac{ \varepsilon_0^p(x)}{ \varepsilon_0(x)}
\end{equation*} 
\begin{equation*} 
\frac{1}{T}= \frac{ \int_{x=0}^{x=L} \sigma_0(x) \varepsilon_0^p(x) A(x) \, dx }{  \int_{x=0}^{x=L} \sigma_0(x) \varepsilon_0(x) A(x) \, dx }=\frac{ \int_{x=0}^{x=L} \frac{1}{t} \sigma_0^2(x) A(x) \, dx}{\int_{x=0}^{x=L} \sigma_0^2(x) A(x) \, dx}     \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{(if E is homogeneous in the structure)}
\end{equation*} 


The above equation ~\eqref{eq:phiiT} can be rewritten as 
\begin{equation}
\left( \varphi-1 \right)T+\phi=0
\label{eq:LaRoche4A} 
\end{equation}

Now, starting from equation ~\eqref{eq:Osgood_modified-C}, we have:
\begin{align} 
\varepsilon(x)  &= \varphi \varepsilon_0(x)  + \phi \varepsilon_0^{p}(x)  \notag \\[3mm]
\varepsilon(x) - \varepsilon_0(x) &=\varepsilon_0(x)  (\varphi-1) \left(1+\frac{\phi}{\varphi-1}\frac{\varepsilon_0^p(x)}{\varepsilon_0(x)}\right) \notag \\[3mm]
\frac{\varepsilon(x)-\varepsilon_0(x)}{ \left(\varphi-1 \right)\varepsilon_0(x)} &= 1+\frac{\phi}{\varphi-1}\frac{\varepsilon_0^p(x)}{\varepsilon_0(x)} \notag \\[3mm]
\frac{E(\varepsilon(x)-\varepsilon_0(x))}{ \left(\varphi-1 \right)\sigma_0(x)} &= 1+\frac{\phi}{\varphi-1}\frac{\varepsilon_0^p(x)}{\varepsilon_0(x)} \notag \\[3mm]
\frac{E(\varepsilon(x)-\varepsilon_0(x))}{ \sigma(x)-\sigma_0(x)} &= 1+\frac{\phi}{\varphi-1}\frac{\varepsilon_0^p(x)}{\varepsilon_0(x)} \notag \\[3mm]
\frac{E(\varepsilon(x)-\varepsilon_0(x))}{ \sigma_0(x)-\sigma(x)} &= -1+\frac{T}{t} \text{             \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \    (from equation ~\eqref{eq:LaRoche4A})  }
\end{align}




Now we define the elastic-follow-up factor $rM=\frac{E(\varepsilon(x) - \varepsilon_0(x)}{ \sigma_0(x)-\sigma(x) }  $, then:
	\begin{equation}
		rM    =\frac{T}{t} -1
		\label{def:EFU}
	\end{equation}

\subsection{Second approach: Generalized stress-strain tensors Ramberg-Osgood under multiaxial configurations}
\label{sec:secondApproach}

In context of a multiaxial configuration, the Ramberg Osgood law is generalized for the plastic deformation tensor:
\begin{align} 
\bar{\bar\varepsilon}^p(\bar{\bar\sigma}) &= \phi(\varphi)  \bar{\bar\varepsilon}^p(\bar{\bar\sigma}_0)  \notag \\[3mm]
\bar{\bar\varepsilon}^p &= \phi(\varphi)  \bar{\bar\varepsilon}_0^p
\label{eq:plastic_tensor}
\end{align}

The total elastoplastic strain tensor is expressed as below:
\begin{equation*} 
\bar{\bar\varepsilon} = \bar{\bar\varepsilon}^{el} +\bar{\bar\varepsilon}^p
\end{equation*}
Following Hooke' law $\bar{\bar\sigma}=\bar{\bar C} \bar{\bar\varepsilon}^{el}$ and equation ~\eqref{eq:plastic_tensor}, we have:
\begin{equation} 
\bar{\bar\varepsilon} =\bar{\bar C}^{-1} \bar{\bar\sigma} +\phi  \bar{\bar\varepsilon}_0^p=\varphi \bar{\bar C}^{-1} \bar{\bar\sigma}_0 +\phi  \bar{\bar\varepsilon}_0^p =\varphi  \bar{\bar\varepsilon}_0 +\phi  \bar{\bar\varepsilon}_0^p
\label{eq:Ramberg-Osgood-modif-2nd}
\end{equation}

Equation ~\eqref{eq:Ramberg-Osgood-modif-2nd} might also be expressed for the equivalent strains:
\begin{align} 
\varepsilon^* =\varphi \varepsilon_0^* + \phi \varepsilon_0^{p*} 
\label{eq:Ramberg-Osgood-modif-2nd-equivalent}
\end{align}


\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figures/energy_balance_equivalent_stress_scheme.png}
\caption{Energy-balance scheme extracted from the presentation, summarising Roche's route from the virtual-work statement of the elastic calculation to the equivalent stress/strain formulation used to define $T$, $t$ and ultimately $r_M$.}
\label{fig:energy-balance-scheme}
\end{figure}




For the elastoplastic analysis (point \textbf{SP}), the local equilibrium equation is given by $\sigma_{ij,j}+f_i=0$ (with $i,j=\overline{1,3}$). \\
Considering an imposed displacement field $\vec u$ prescribed on the boundary of the domain, together with a thermal expansion load $\bar{\bar\epsilon}$ acting on all material points (see Figure \ref{fig:energy-balance-scheme}), application of the divergence theorem and the principle of virtual work leads to the following relation involving an infinitesimal variation of the stress field, $\delta\sigma$:
\begin{equation}
\iiint \delta\bar{\bar\sigma}:\bar{\bar\varepsilon}\,dV = \iint \delta\bar{\bar\sigma}\,\vec n\,\vec u\,dS - \iiint \delta\bar{\bar\sigma}:\bar{\bar\epsilon}\,dV
\label{eq:roche6}
\end{equation}
Using relation~\eqref{eq:kachanov-stress-equivalent} and introducing the variation of the unknown field, $\delta\varphi$, yields 
\begin{equation*} \iiint_V (\delta\varphi)\,\bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\varepsilon}}\,dV = \iint_S (\delta\varphi)\,\bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 \cdot \vec{n} \cdot \vec{u}\,dS - \iiint_V (\delta\varphi)\,\bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\epsilon}}\,dV
\end{equation*}


\begin{equation} \iiint_V \bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\varepsilon}}\,dV = \iint_S \bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 \cdot \vec{n} \cdot \vec{u}\,dS - \iiint_V\bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\epsilon}}\,dV
\label{eq:EnergyBalance-elastoplastic-2nd}
\end{equation}


For the elastic solution (point \textbf{E}), consider the same secondary loads ($\vec{u}$ and $\bar{\bar{\epsilon}}$). The principle of virtual work gives 
\begin{equation} 
\frac{1}{2}\iiint_V \bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\varepsilon}}_0 \, dV = \frac{1}{2}\iint_S \bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 \cdot \vec{n} \cdot \vec{u} \, dS - \frac{1}{2}\iiint_V \bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\epsilon}} \, dV \label{eq:EnergyBalance-elastic-2nd}
\end{equation} 

The energy balances for the elastic solution (Equation~\eqref{eq:EnergyBalance-elastic-2nd}) and the elastoplastic solution (Equation~\eqref{eq:EnergyBalance-elastoplastic-2nd}) yield \begin{flalign*} 
\iiint_V \bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\varepsilon}} \, dV &= \iiint_V \bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\varepsilon}}_0 \, dV, \\ \iiint_V \frac{1}{\varphi}\, \bar{\bar{\sigma}} : \bar{\bar{\varepsilon}} \, dV &= \iiint_V \bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\varepsilon}}_0 \, dV
\end{flalign*} 

Neglecting the contribution of volumetric strains to the strain energy, the above tensorial relation can be expressed in terms of the equivalent stress and equivalent strain as
\begin{flalign*}
\iiint_V \frac{1}{\varphi}\, \sigma^* \varepsilon^* \, dV = \iiint_V \sigma_0^* \varepsilon_0^* \, dV
\end{flalign*} 

Substituting Equations~\eqref{eq:Ramberg-Osgood-modif-2nd-equivalent} and ~\eqref{eq:kachanov-stress-equivalent} into the above expression yields 
\begin{flalign*} 
\iiint_V \sigma_0^* \left(  \varphi \varepsilon_0^* + \phi \varepsilon_0^{p*}  \right) \, dV &= \iiint_V \sigma_0^* \varepsilon_0^* \, dV
\end{flalign*} 
\begin{flalign} 
\left( \varphi-1 \right) \iiint_V \sigma_0^* \varepsilon_0^* \, dV + \phi \iiint_V \sigma_0^* \varepsilon_0^{p*} \, dV &=0
\label{eq:phiiT-2nd}
\end{flalign} 



Defining the local resilience t and global resilience T as below:
\begin{equation*} 
\frac{1}{t}= \frac{ \varepsilon_0^{p*}}{ \varepsilon_0^*}
\end{equation*} 
\begin{equation*} 
\frac{1}{T}= \frac{ \iiint_V \sigma_0^* \varepsilon_0^{p*} \, dV }{ \iiint_V \sigma_0^* \varepsilon_0^* \, dV }=\frac{ \iiint_V \frac{1}{t} \sigma_0^{*2} \, dV }{ \iiint_V \sigma_0^{*2}  \, dV }    \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \text{(if E is homogeneous in the structure)}
\end{equation*} 


The above equation \eqref{eq:phiiT-2nd} can be rewritten as 
\begin{equation}\left( \varphi-1 \right)T+\phi=0
\label{eq:LaRoche4} 
\end{equation}


Now, starting from equation ~\eqref{eq:Ramberg-Osgood-modif-2nd-equivalent}, we have:
\begin{align} 
\varepsilon^* &= \varphi \varepsilon_0^* + \phi \varepsilon_0^{p*}  \notag \\[3mm]
\varepsilon^* - \varepsilon_0^* &=\varepsilon_0^*  \left(\varphi-1 \right) \left(1+\frac{\phi}{\varphi-1}\frac{\varepsilon_0^{p*}}{\varepsilon_0^*}\right) \notag \\[3mm]
\frac{\varepsilon^*-\varepsilon_0^*}{ \left(\varphi-1 \right)\varepsilon_0^*} &= 1+\frac{\phi}{\varphi-1}\frac{\varepsilon_0^{p*}}{\varepsilon_0^*} \notag \\[3mm]
\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \frac{\varepsilon^*-\varepsilon_0^*} { (\varphi-1) \left( \frac{2}{3}\frac{1+\nu}{E}  \sigma_0^* \right) }  &= 1+\frac{\phi}{\varphi-1}\frac{\varepsilon_0^{p*}}{\varepsilon_0^*} \notag  \qquad \text{(from equation \eqref{e_final}, see Appendix \ref{sec:equivalent_sigma_epsilon_eq})} \notag \\[3mm]
\frac{E(\varepsilon^*-\varepsilon_0^*)} { (\varphi-1) \sigma_0^*} \frac{3}{2(1+\nu)} &= 1 +\frac{\phi}{\varphi-1}\frac{\varepsilon_0^{p*}}{\varepsilon_0^*}  \notag  \\[3mm]
\frac{E(\varepsilon^*-\varepsilon_0^*)} {\sigma_0^*-\sigma^*} &=0.867 \left( -1-\frac{\phi}{\varphi-1}\frac{\varepsilon_0^{p*}}{\varepsilon_0^*} \right) \notag \\[3mm]
\frac{E(\varepsilon^*-\varepsilon_0^*)} {\sigma_0^*-\sigma^*} &=  0.867 \left( \frac{T}{t} -1      \right)
\end{align}

Now we define the elastic-follow-up factor $rM=\frac{E(\varepsilon^* - \varepsilon_0^*)}{ \sigma_0^*-\sigma^* }  $, then:
	\begin{equation}
		rM    =0.867\left( \frac{T}{t} -1      \right) < \frac{T}{t} -1
		\label{def:EFU-2nd}
	\end{equation}

\subsection{Third approach: Equivalent stress--strain relation Ramberg-Osgood relation under multiaxial configurations}
\label{sec:thirdApproach}





For multidirection loads, the Ramberg-Osgood relation is generalized as below:
\begin{equation}
\varepsilon^* = \frac{\sigma^*}{E}   + \left(\frac{\sigma^*}{K}\right)^{1/n}
\label{eq:Osgood}
\end{equation}



By converting the tensorial relation of Equation~\ref{eq:kachanov-stress} into its equivalent stress--strain form, the Ramberg--Osgood relation can be rewritten as follows:
\begin{align} 
\varepsilon^* &= \frac{\varphi \sigma_0^*}{E} + \left(\frac{\varphi \sigma_0^*}{K}\right)^{1/n} \notag \\
&= \varphi \frac{3\varepsilon_0^*}{2(1+\nu)} + \varphi^{1/n} \left(\frac{\sigma_0^*}{K}\right)^{1/n} \notag \\
&= \varphi \frac{3\varepsilon_0^*}{2(1+\nu)}  + \phi \varepsilon_0^{p*} 
\label{eq:Osgood_modified}
\end{align}

With $\varepsilon_0^{p*} $ is the equivalent plastic deformation obtained by Ramberg-Osgood law using the elastic stress $\sigma_0^*$. \\








For the elastoplastic analysis (point \textbf{SP}), the local equilibrium equation is given by $\sigma_{ij,j}+f_i=0$ (with $i,j=\overline{1,3}$). \\
Considering an imposed displacement field $\vec u$ prescribed on the boundary of the domain, together with a thermal expansion load $\bar{\bar\epsilon}$ acting on all material points (see Figure \ref{fig:energy-balance-scheme}), application of the divergence theorem and the principle of virtual work leads to the following relation involving an infinitesimal variation of the stress field, $\delta\sigma$:
\begin{equation}
\iiint \delta\bar{\bar\sigma}:\bar{\bar\varepsilon}\,dV = \iint \delta\bar{\bar\sigma}\,\vec n\,\vec u\,dS - \iiint \delta\bar{\bar\sigma}:\bar{\bar\epsilon}\,dV
\label{eq:roche6}
\end{equation}
Using relation~\eqref{eq:kachanov-stress} and introducing the variation of the unknown field, $\delta\varphi$, yields 
\begin{equation*} \iiint_V (\delta\varphi)\,\bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\varepsilon}}\,dV = \iint_S (\delta\varphi)\,\bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 \cdot \vec{n} \cdot \vec{u}\,dS - \iiint_V (\delta\varphi)\,\bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\epsilon}}\,dV
\end{equation*}


\begin{equation} \iiint_V \bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\varepsilon}}\,dV = \iint_S \bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 \cdot \vec{n} \cdot \vec{u}\,dS - \iiint_V\bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\epsilon}}\,dV
\label{eq:EnergyBalance-elastoplastic}
\end{equation}


For the elastic solution (point \textbf{E}), consider the same secondary loads ($\vec{u}$ and $\bar{\bar{\epsilon}}$). The principle of virtual work gives 
\begin{equation} 
\frac{1}{2}\iiint_V \bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\varepsilon}}_0 \, dV = \frac{1}{2}\iint_S \bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 \cdot \vec{n} \cdot \vec{u} \, dS - \frac{1}{2}\iiint_V \bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\epsilon}} \, dV \label{eq:EnergyBalance-elastic}
\end{equation} 

The energy balances for the elastic solution (Equation~\eqref{eq:EnergyBalance-elastic}) and the elastoplastic solution (Equation~\eqref{eq:EnergyBalance-elastoplastic}) yield \begin{flalign*} 
\iiint_V \bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\varepsilon}} \, dV &= \iiint_V \bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\varepsilon}}_0 \, dV, \\ \iiint_V \frac{1}{\varphi}\, \bar{\bar{\sigma}} : \bar{\bar{\varepsilon}} \, dV &= \iiint_V \bar{\bar{\sigma}}_0 : \bar{\bar{\varepsilon}}_0 \, dV
\end{flalign*} 

Neglecting the contribution of volumetric strains to the strain energy, the above tensorial relation can be expressed in terms of the equivalent stress and equivalent strain as
\begin{flalign*}
\iiint_V \frac{1}{\varphi}\, \sigma^* \varepsilon^* \, dV = \iiint_V \sigma_0^* \varepsilon_0^* \, dV
\end{flalign*} 

Substituting Equations~\eqref{eq:kachanov-stress} and ~\eqref{eq:Osgood_modified} into the above expression yields 
\begin{flalign*} 
\iiint_V \sigma_0^* \left(  \varphi \frac{3\varepsilon_0^*}{2(1+\nu)}  + \phi \varepsilon_0^{p*}  \right) \, dV &= \iiint_V \sigma_0^* \varepsilon_0^* \, dV
\end{flalign*} 
\begin{flalign*} 
\left( \varphi\frac{3}{2(1+\nu)}-1 \right) \iiint_V \sigma_0^* \varepsilon_0^* \, dV + \phi \iiint_V \sigma_0^* \varepsilon_0^{p*} \, dV &=0
\end{flalign*} 




Defining the global resilience T as below:
\begin{equation*} 
\frac{1}{T}= \frac{ \iiint_V \sigma_0^* \varepsilon_0^{p*} \, dV }{ \iiint_V \sigma_0^* \varepsilon_0^* \, dV }
\end{equation*} 
The above equation can be rewritten as 
\begin{equation}\left( \varphi\frac{3}{2(1+\nu)}-1 \right)T+\phi=0
\label{eq:LaRoche4} 
\end{equation}


Now, starting from equation ~\eqref{eq:Osgood_modified}, we have:
\begin{align} 
\varepsilon^* &= \varphi \frac{3\varepsilon_0^*}{2(1+\nu)}  + \phi \varepsilon_0^{p*}  \notag \\[3mm]
\varepsilon^* - \varepsilon_0^* &=\varepsilon_0^*  \left(\varphi\frac{3}{2(1+\nu)}-1 \right) \left(1+\frac{\phi}{\varphi\frac{3}{2(1+\nu)}-1}\frac{\varepsilon_0^{p*}}{\varepsilon_0^*}\right) \notag \\[3mm]
\frac{\varepsilon^*-\varepsilon_0^*}{ \left(\varphi\frac{3}{2(1+\nu)}-1 \right)\varepsilon_0^*} &= 1+\frac{\phi}{\varphi\frac{3}{2(1+\nu)}-1}\frac{\varepsilon_0^{p*}}{\varepsilon_0^*} \notag \\[3mm]
\ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \ \frac{\varepsilon^*-\varepsilon_0^*} { \left(\varphi\frac{3}{2(1+\nu)}-1 \right) \left( \frac{2}{3}\frac{1+\nu}{E}  \sigma_0^* \right) }  &= 1+\frac{\phi}{\varphi\frac{3}{2(1+\nu)}-1}\frac{\varepsilon_0^{p*}}{\varepsilon_0^*} \qquad \text{(from equation \eqref{e_final}, see Appendix \ref{sec:equivalent_sigma_epsilon_eq})} \notag \\[3mm]
\frac{E(\varepsilon^*-\varepsilon_0^*)} {\varphi \sigma_0^*- \frac{2(1+\nu)}{3}\sigma_0^*} &= 1+\frac{\phi}{\varphi\frac{3}{2(1+\nu)}-1}\frac{\varepsilon_0^{p*}}{\varepsilon_0^*} \notag  \\[3mm]
\frac{E(\varepsilon^*-\varepsilon_0^*)} {0.867\sigma_0^*-\sigma^*} &= -1-\frac{\phi}{\varphi\frac{3}{2(1+\nu)}-1}\frac{\varepsilon_0^{p*}}{\varepsilon_0^*} \notag
\end{align}



With the local resilience defined as $t=\frac{\varepsilon_0^*}{\varepsilon_0^{p*}}$ and using equation ~\eqref{eq:LaRoche4}, the above formula becomes:
	\begin{equation*}
		\frac{E(\varepsilon^*-\varepsilon_0^*)} {0.867\sigma_0^*-\sigma^*} =    \frac{T}{t} -1      
	\end{equation*}
Now we define the elastic-follow-up factor $rM=\frac{E(\varepsilon^* - \varepsilon_0^*)}{ \sigma_0^*-\sigma^* }  $, then:
\begin{equation*}
	rM    < \frac{E(\varepsilon^*-\varepsilon_0^*)} {0.867\sigma_0^*-\sigma^*} 
\end{equation*}
or 
\begin{equation}
	rM    <  \frac{T}{t}-1 
	\label{def:EFU-3rd}
\end{equation}

\sigma_{0,spring} = \frac{k_{total}*u}{S_{spring}} = \frac{65.703 kN/mm * 4.869mm}{5086.66 mm^2}=63~\mathrm{MPa}
\end{equation*}
\begin{equation*}
1/t = 0
\end{equation*}


Global resilience and the elastic follow-up factor:
\begin{equation*}
T=\frac{\frac{\sigma_{0,section}}{E}*S*L+\frac{\sigma_{0,spring}}{E/8}*S_{spring}*L_{spring}}{\sigma_{0,section}*\varepsilon_M^p(\sigma_{0,section})*L*S}=0.261
\end{equation*}
\begin{equation*}
r_M=\frac{T}{t}-1=2
\end{equation*}


Now, to estimate the elastoplastic solution, we resolve a system of two equations:
\begin{equation*} 
\left\{ \begin{aligned} 
\varepsilon &= \frac{\sigma}{E} + 0.01 \left( \frac{\sigma}{C_0\,R_{p0.2,\mathrm{mean}}} \right)^{\frac{1}{n_0}} \\[1em]
\frac{\dfrac{\sigma_0}{E}-\varepsilon} {\sigma_0-\sigma} &= -\frac{r_M}{E} 
\end{aligned} \right.
\end{equation*}
The elastoplastic solution found is :  $\sigma=93.143\mathrm{MPa}$ and $\varepsilon=0.124 \%$. \\
The elastoplastic solution found by MSC Marc: $\sigma_{Marc}=90.3\mathrm{MPa}$ and $\varepsilon_{Marc}=0.13 \%$.





\newpage
\textcolor{red}{to redo these contents}


The factor $r_M$ has the following limiting behaviour:
\begin{itemize}
    \item $r_M = 0$ ($T=t$): no elastic follow-up; the stress is purely secondary (fully self-limiting).
    \item $r_M \to \infty$ ($T \gg t$): very significant elastic follow-up; the stress behaves as purely primary.
\end{itemize}
Note that a \emph{higher} $r_M$ is treated by the code as a \emph{more conservative}, more primary-like, assumption; taking $r_M(\mathrm{coeff}=1) > r_M(\mathrm{coeff}=0.867)$ is therefore itself a conservative simplification.

In the Roche three-bar analogy, Bar 2 (no spring) corresponds to $r_M=0$, Bar 1 (rigid connection) corresponds to $r_M\to\infty$, and Bar 3 (elastic spring) lies at an intermediate, finite value of $r_M$ that depends on the spring stiffness $k_2$.

\section{Definition of equivalent stress and strain}
\label{sec:equivalent_sigma_epsilon_eq}

The equivalent stress and equivalent strain for the purely elastic solution are defined as 
\begin{equation} 
\varepsilon_0^* = \sqrt{\frac{2}{3}e_{0ij}e_{0ij}}, \qquad e_{0ij} = \varepsilon_{0ij} - \frac{1}{3}\varepsilon_{0kk}\delta_{ij}, \label{eij} 
\end{equation} 
and 
\begin{equation} 
\sigma_0^* = \sqrt{\frac{3}{2}s_{0ij}s_{0ij}}, \qquad s_{0ij} = \sigma_{0ij} - \frac{1}{3}\sigma_{0kk}\delta_{ij}. \label{sij} 
\end{equation} 
Using Hooke's law $\varepsilon_{0ij} = \frac{1+\nu}{E}\sigma_{0ij} - \frac{\nu}{E}\sigma_{0kk}\delta_{ij} $, and consider the trace of Hook's tensorial relation, we have:  
\begin{equation*} 
\varepsilon_{0kk} = \frac{1+\nu}{E}\sigma_{0kk} - 3\frac{\nu}{E}\sigma_{0kk} = \frac{1-2\nu}{E}\sigma_{0kk}
\end{equation*} 
Substituting the trace and tensorial relation of the Hook law into Equation~\eqref{eij} yields 
\begin{equation*} 
e_{0ij} = \frac{1+\nu}{E}\sigma_{0ij} - \frac{\nu}{E}\sigma_{0kk}\delta_{ij} - \frac{1}{3}\frac{1-2\nu}{E}\sigma_{0kk}\delta_{ij}. \label{eij3} 
\end{equation*} 
After simplification, 
\begin{equation*} 
e_{0ij} = \frac{1+\nu}{E} \left( \sigma_{0ij} - \frac{1}{3}\sigma_{0kk}\delta_{ij} \right) = \frac{1+\nu}{E}s_{0ij}. 
\end{equation*} 
Using the above relation, equations~\eqref{eij} and \eqref{sij}, the equivalent strain is related to the equivalent stress by 
\begin{equation} 
\varepsilon_0^* = \frac{2}{3}\frac{1+\nu}{E}\sigma_0^*. 
\label{e_final} 
\end{equation}

\subsection{Analytical validation on a cantilever with imposed end displacement}

To validate the production-code implementation of $r_M$ and $g$ independently of the elbow benchmark, a simple, closed-form case is considered: a straight cantilever beam of length $l$, clamped at one end, subjected to an imposed transverse displacement $\Delta u$ at the free end (Figure~\ref{fig:cantilever-sketch}). In the benchmark slide deck, the imposed displacement is specified explicitly as $\Delta u=\SI{12}{mm}$. In the closed-form derivation below, the curvilinear abscissa $x$ is measured from the free end ($x=0$) towards the clamp ($x=l$), so that the elastic bending moment and the elastic reference stress vary linearly with $x$.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figures/cantilever_sketch.png}
\caption{Schematic of the cantilever validation case: a beam of length $l$, clamped at one end and subjected to an imposed transverse displacement $\Delta u$ at the other; for the analytical derivation, $x$ is measured from the free end to the clamped end.}
\label{fig:cantilever-sketch}
\end{figure}

For this configuration, the elastic reference stress is linear in $x$, $\sigma_{\mathrm{ref}}(x) = \omega\,x$ with $\omega = 0.79\,(3EI/l^{3})\,\Delta u/Z$, and the local resilience $1/t(x)$ follows directly from the Ramberg--Osgood plastic strain $\varepsilon_{M,p}$. Carrying out the integrals of Eq.~\eqref{eq:Tglobal} in closed form for this linear stress distribution gives the elastic follow-up factor as an explicit function of position,
\begin{equation}
r(x) \;=\; \frac{T}{t}-1 \;=\; \frac{1+2n_0}{3n_0}\left(\frac{x}{l}\right)^{\frac{1-n_0}{n_0}} - 1 ,
\label{eq:cantilever-rx}
\end{equation}
which is monotonically increasing towards the clamp and is therefore maximised at the clamped end ($x=l$), giving the closed-form bound
\begin{equation}
r_{\max} \;=\; \frac{1-n_0}{3n_0} .
\label{eq:cantilever-rmax}
\end{equation}
For the benchmark material ($n_0=0.1125$), the same closed-form development gives the global resilience quantity $T=0.444$, and Eqs.~\eqref{eq:cantilever-rx}--\eqref{eq:cantilever-rmax} then give $r_{\max}=2.63$. Using the no-pressure RCC-MRx expression for the abatement coefficient on that analytical solution yields $g=0.3118$, i.e.\ essentially the same value as the production-code result $g=0.3116$ reported below. Figures~\ref{fig:cantilever-3d}, \ref{fig:cantilever-vm} and \ref{fig:hexagon-cantilever-table} show, respectively, the FE model, the non-linear stress field and the production-code output used to validate this closed-form result.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.5\textwidth]{figures/cantilever_3d_model.png}
\caption{Finite-element model of the cantilever validation case: clamped at one end (yellow anchor), imposed displacement enforced through a rigid link at the free end (yellow cones).}
\label{fig:cantilever-3d}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering

\caption{Elasto-plastic MSC~Marc result for the simplified validation benchmark used to check the $r_M/g$ implementation: the equivalent von Mises field confirms the stress localisation pattern that is then compared against the elastic-plus-RCC-MRx route.}
\label{fig:cantilever-vm}
\end{figure}

\begin{figure}[htbp]
